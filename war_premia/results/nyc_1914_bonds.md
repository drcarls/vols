# NYC bonds, 1914: the closure and the reopening (descriptive)

How the New York bond market reacted after war began — from the *Commercial and
Financial Chronicle* (a New York paper; public domain, via FRASER / Wayback).
Descriptive and sourced: every figure in `data/nyc_1914_bonds.csv` carries its OCR
quote. It is a **different mechanism** from the London/belligerent case, so it is
kept as its own panel.

## The arc

**Outbreak — a forced-selling / gold event, not a war-risk repricing.** The US was
a *debtor*; Europe held vast quantities of American securities and dumped them for
gold. The Chronicle (1 Aug 1914): "the liquidation was much more important than
mere selling of floating supplies of American stocks held in Europe"; gold
"engagements for export this week … aggregated **$41,850,000**." On Friday 31 July,
"before the opening of business, [they] decided to close the Exchange
indefinitely," which "prevents … further security sales here on foreign account."
The closure's purpose was to stop European liquidation and the gold drain — the US
defending its gold, not a belligerent protecting its own debt. US Governments held
near par in the last sessions (2s at 97; Panama 3s at 101¾).

**Closure (~4 months).** Managed, not priced: Aldrich-Vreeland emergency currency,
a gold pool, the cotton loan fund, and a restricted minimum-price regime. The
Aug–Nov quotes are administered floors — don't read them (same discipline as the
London closure).

**Reopening (bonds first, Sat 28 Nov 1914) — the genuinely observable reaction.**
The Chronicle (5 Dec 1914): the Exchange "opened its doors on Saturday last
(Nov. 28) for trading in bonds. There were no formal ceremonies and after the
first burst of enthusiasm trading under the new conditions started without a
hitch." "The bond market is again fairly active." Minimum prices were posted
below which no trade was allowed.

## The reopening prices — high grades came back firm, near par

From the NYSE bond range table for the reopening week (28 Nov – 4 Dec 1914):

| bond | low | high | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **US Steel s f 5s 1963** | 99¾ | 100¼ | the week's most-active bond ($494k) |
| **US Rubber 6s 1918** | 100⅞ | 101⅞ | *above* par |
| Bethlehem Steel 5s 1926 | 97 | 98¾ | war supplier |
| Central Leather 5s 1925 | 96 | 97¾ | |
| Interboro RT 5s 1966 | 96 | 97 | |
| short high-grades (Erie '15, Int Harv '15, AT&T Sub '16) | ~99 | ~100¼ | at par |

Actual first-day trade, verifiable to the tick: **Chicago City Ry 5s, "Nov. 28,
$3,000 at 98@98½."**

## The finding

**NYC bonds did not crash after war started.** The would-be crash — a European
fire-sale for gold — was *pre-empted* by closing the exchange on 31 July; and when
bonds actually traded again four months later they reopened **firm, near par, and
orderly**, the high grades within a point or two of 100. The New York shock was a
**gold/liquidity event driven by the US's debtor position**, contained by policy,
not a repricing of American credit for war risk. Within months, as war orders
poured in and the US turned creditor, prices recovered into the 1915 boom — the
pivot Silber (*When Washington Shut Down Wall Street*) reads as the start of US
financial primacy.

Caveat kept from the London audit: the Aug–Nov closure quotes are minimum-price
artifacts and are excluded. The 28 Nov reopening is a genuine, observable event;
the observation is that US bonds came back firm.
