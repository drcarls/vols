# SPENT
### Vienna, winter 1912

*Magazine version, companion to "The Squeeze." The working evidence is in `crisis_deviation_five_cases.md`, `continental_press_warscares.md`, and `lansburgh_other_crises_1914.md`. Notes at the end carry what the prose sets aside.*

---

In the last week of October 1912 the Austro-Hungarian Bank raised its discount rate for the second time in a fortnight. It had stood at five per cent on the twelfth; on the twenty-sixth it went to five and a half; five weeks later, at the end of November, to six — three moves in seven weeks, with no failed bank behind them, no crash, no harvest gone wrong. What there was instead was a war four hundred miles to the south in which the Monarchy was not a combatant, and a growing chance that it would become one.

Of all the pre-war crises, this is the one where the money market bit hardest. At Agadir the evidence is quantities, and the price of money barely moved once the quarter-end was accounted for. Here the price moved, in the city with the most to fear, and it moved the way a market moves when it is pricing not a quarrel but the chance the quarrel goes general.

---

## I.

Two wars were fought in the Balkans in less than a year, and Europe's money markets treated them as opposite events — which is the argument of this chapter in miniature.

In October 1912 the Balkan League — Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro — fell on the Ottoman Empire together and in a few weeks destroyed its position in Europe. Serbia doubled in size. A new Albania had to be invented at a conference to keep Serbia off the Adriatic, because the one outcome Vienna would not accept was a Serbian port on the sea it regarded as its own. This First Balkan War frightened the great powers precisely because the men who had started it could not contain it: a bigger Serbia was a magnet for the South Slavs inside the Dual Monarchy, and a beaten Serbia was a client Russia could not abandon a second time, having abandoned it once over Bosnia in 1909. Between a Vienna that would not let Serbia grow and a St Petersburg that would not let it be crushed lay the mechanism by which a local victory could become a continental war.

The Second Balkan War the following summer was the opposite kind of event. The victors fell out over the spoils; Bulgaria attacked its former allies and lost to all of them at once, Romania and the Turks included. It was brutal and brief and it settled a map, but it threatened no great power's vital interest, and the alliances had no reason to run. In the exact sense the money market cared about, it was a *local* war.

The market read the difference. It watched the second war, an actual war with actual battles, with something close to indifference, and it had priced the first months earlier, before any great power fired a shot — because it was never pricing the violence, only whether the system would hold.

---

## II.

The state with the most exposure to that question was the one least able to pay for the answer.

Austria-Hungary was neither poor nor small: a great power of fifty million people, with heavy industry, a respectable army, and a currency as good as gold. What it lacked was a treasury that could act. The Monarchy was two states under one crown, and its common affairs — army, navy, foreign office — were funded by a budget that Austria and Hungary renegotiated, with mutual ill will, every ten years. The joint finance minister, Leon von Biliński, presided over an arrangement in which every florin for a common purpose had to be pried from two parliaments that agreed on little except their reluctance to pay. A state built to decide slowly and fund grudgingly was about to be asked how fast it could put six hundred thousand men into Galicia and hold them there through a winter.

Pressing against that machinery was Conrad von Hötzendorf, the chief of the general staff, who had spent years urging a preventive war — against Italy, against Serbia, against whichever neighbour was weakest that season — and who saw in the Balkan collapse the opportunity he had been demanding. He was reinstated as chief of staff in December 1912, as the crisis deepened, and his bureau kept a war plan ready for each contingency: Case I against Italy, Case R against Russia, Case B against Serbia. Conrad wanted to march on Serbia while it was still muddy with its own victory, and he was not allowed to. Why he was not allowed to is the subject of this chapter, and the reasons are only partly military.

---

## III.

Because Vienna would not let Serbia reach the sea and Russia would not let Vienna dictate to Serbia, both empires began, in the last weeks of 1912, to mobilise against each other along the Galician frontier. Neither wanted the war the movement implied. Both knew that armies once assembled are expensive to keep and humiliating to send home, and that the side which stood down first would be seen to have blinked.

So neither stood down. Austria-Hungary put its Galician corps on a war footing and reinforced them; Russia held a "trial mobilisation" in the districts facing them; and the two watched each other across the frontier from the winter of 1912 into the spring of 1913 — no battle, no ultimatum, no war, only several hundred thousand men under arms on each side and a bill that grew every week they stayed there. The Monarchy later struck a medal for it, the Mobilization Cross 1912/13, awarded to the men called up in that autumn who served at least four weeks: a standing mobilisation long enough and large enough to warrant its own decoration.

A vigil of that kind only drains. A war at least ends — in victory, defeat, or a treaty — and returns the men to the fields; a mobilisation that fights nobody spends the money and hands back nothing. Austria-Hungary paid, through that winter, roughly what a short victorious campaign might have cost, and got no territory, no indemnity, no settled frontier, no war it could point to — only a demobilisation order in the spring and a hole in the common budget that the delegations would spend the next year refusing to fill.

---

## IV.

What the market made of it can be read, because Vienna kept a financial press equal to the occasion.

The London trade papers, on which most later accounts of the continental money markets rest, carried the Vienna rate as a single figure — "Vienna 6%" — and moved on. The *Neue Freie Presse*, Vienna's own paper, carried the market as it actually behaved. On the thirtieth of November 1912, at the height of the mobilisation, its money article gave not one number but three: *Bankzinsfuß 6 Prozent, Privatdiskont 5½ Prozent, längere Sichten 6 bis 6¼ Prozent* — bank rate six, private discount five and a half, longer bills six to six and a quarter. That is a term structure, and an inverted one: money for a few weeks cost more than the central bank's own charge, and money for longer cost more still — the mark of a market that expects the strain to last, and exactly the detail the London single-figure reporting erased. The Anglophone record of the continental money markets is a compression of the original; read Vienna in Vienna and the crisis is in the spread, read it in London and it is one flat number that never splits.

The private discount had run near four per cent before the war and stood at five and a half in the last week of November, while the Austro-Hungarian Bank's own rate climbed five, five and a half, six across October and November. The tell that this was a war scare and not a money panic is what moved the bourse: it firmed on political news, rising on the report *daß es wegen der schwebenden politischen Fragen nicht zu einem ernsten Zerwürfnis unter den Großstaaten kommen werde* — that the pending questions would not bring a serious rupture among the great powers. The market rose when the war looked less likely and tightened when it looked more, pricing the thing directly.

---

## V.

The strain did not stay in Vienna. It ran north into Germany, where Alfred Lansburgh counted it in *Die Bank* as he had counted the autumn of 1911, under a title that says the whole of it: *Spargelder und Kriegsflucht* — savings deposits and the flight from war. Savers had stormed the banks and savings banks to withdraw or give notice on their balances, and for a time it looked as though the Reichsbank might have to raise again a discount rate it had only lately put up to six per cent. The withdrawals were heaviest in East Prussia, the province that would be first across the Russian frontier if the frontier moved — heavy enough that the Chancellor sent a calming telegram to its senior official and the press opened a campaign against hoarding. The January sequel, *Geldklemme*, recorded the squeeze continuing, with the Reichsbank's president, who had warned for two years that too much credit rested on too little cash, reduced to refusing the worst bills and tightening terms on the rest.

The autumn of 1911 had not been unique, then. The same mechanism — a war fear turning into a deposit run, a deposit run into central-bank strain — worked again in the winter of 1912, tilted this time to the east, toward Russia, because that was where the danger lay.

---

## VI.

In Berlin, on the eighth of December 1912, the men who ran the German war machine met and, in effect, asked for more time.

The meeting has become famous as a "war council," and historians have argued for a century over how much to make of it. Its shape, at least, is not in dispute. Present were the Kaiser, Moltke of the general staff, and the navy chiefs Tirpitz, Müller and Heeringen. Moltke argued that if war was coming it had better come soon. Tirpitz asked instead, in the phrase the record preserves, for "a postponement of the great fight for one and a half years," because the navy was not ready for a war that included Britain; his stated prerequisites were the widening of the Kiel Canal and the U-boat base at Heligoland. John Röhl has pointed out that the canal's widening was due for completion in the summer of 1914 — while cautioning that the report shows no agreement on a war in that year. Whatever else the meeting was, it was a room of powerful men agreeing a great war was coming and that the machine was not yet built to fight it, and the request that came out of it was for delay on the grounds of an armourer, not a diplomat.

The point for this chapter is narrow. It is not that finance stopped the war in December 1912; the "material" at issue there was naval and engineering, not fiscal. It is that the men closest to the decision, on both sides of the Austro-German alliance, kept finding the material unready — and that eighteen months later, in a July arranged to be brief, no one made that argument at all.

---

## VII.

The money-market case for this crisis is real, but it is not enormous, and the difference has to be stated plainly.

Run the Balkan tightening through the same seasonal control used on every other crisis in the study — measure each city's rate against what that city normally does in those weeks — and the war signal, once the ordinary autumn stringency and the tight cyclical level of 1912 are removed, is modest. So is every pre-war signal: detrended, none is a dramatic squeeze, and the large raw numbers turn out to be mostly the business cycle. What survives the control is not magnitude but timing — markets tightening together, in the same week, on the event rather than the calendar — together with one change that can be isolated to a single country.

That country is Austria. Its spread over the safe benchmark had fallen year after year as it became, like everyone else, a better peacetime borrower; across the mobilisation it broke that decline and rose — a little over one half, to seven tenths, to a full point — above every control year in the sample. Of the five great powers it is the only one whose price of risk visibly turns at this crisis and does not turn back, which makes it the cleanest case in the period.

The qualification that keeps it honest is that the Austrian spread builds slowly, across the whole winter, rather than jumping before any decision. That is the signature of accumulating cost — a state paying, week after week, for an army it cannot use — not of a market vetoing a choice in advance. The evidence supports the sentence *Austria paid, and the paying showed*; it does not support *the market stopped Austria from fighting*, and this chapter claims only the first.

The summer of 1913 is the trap the subject sets, so it is worth disarming. The Second Balkan War throws up a large, synchronised spike in the rate series, and it is tempting to read it as the war. It is not: it falls in the last days of June, on the semester-end settlement, and the contemporary press says so. *Le Temps*, reporting Paris on the thirtieth of June 1913, met the war's news with money abundant, rates moderate, and the market greeting it all *avec calme et même indifférence*. The great-power money market ignored an actual Balkan war, having tightened months earlier for a mobilisation that produced none — which is not a flaw in the data but the finding itself.

---

## VIII.

What the vigil cost is the question the chapter cannot yet fully answer, and the honest course is to say so rather than reach past the evidence.

That Austria-Hungary mobilised twice in the crisis, held those forces at great expense, fought nobody, and stood them down is established, as is the wound the common budget carried afterward, which the delegations quarrelled over. What is not established, because it lives in Austro-Hungarian budget and delegation records this project has not yet worked through, is the precise figure — what the mobilisation cost, how it was funded, and whether Biliński ever set down in writing what the ledger implies: that the Monarchy had spent on a bluff the reserve it would need for the real thing. Until those records are read, the strongest empirical case in the book rests on a price series that turned and a cost documented in its effects but not yet in its amount. That is where it is honestly left.

---

## IX.

What can be said is what happened next, and it fits.

Of the five great powers, Austria-Hungary is the one that never closed the gap its crisis exposed. France, squeezed over Russian bonds in 1905, spent the next decade tightening its alliance and its finances. Germany, made to feel its illiquidity in 1911, went to work on the vault and had the reform finished by the month the war began. Austria-Hungary mobilised, paid, and emerged weaker, its common treasury more contested than before and its military machine shown to be assemblable but not affordable twice.

So when the crisis it had been rehearsing finally came, it could not act on its own account. In July 1914 the Monarchy that wanted the war with Serbia could not be sure of paying for the wider war that war would bring — and it did not have to be, because the guarantee came from Berlin. The blank cheque of the fifth of July was several things at once: a strategic guarantee, a diplomatic licence, and, on the reading this book offers, a partial underwriting — the stronger partner assuming the risk the weaker one had discovered, in the winter of 1912, that it could not carry alone.

The market had said as much, in its own vocabulary, two years earlier. It ignored two Balkan wars, because wars that stay local do not threaten the system, and it priced a winter of mobilisation that ended in a demobilisation order, because that was the winter the system nearly ran. It was never pricing which country would fight, only whether this quarrel would be the one that went general — and in the winter of 1912 it judged, correctly, that this one would not, and charged Austria-Hungary for the fright. Eighteen months later it had no chance to charge anyone, because the war was arranged to be too quick for that. But the state that walked into it least able to pay was the one that had already spent its war, and got none, in a Galician winter two years before.

## NOTES

*What the prose above sets aside. Every claim that is contested, uncorroborated, or unverified appears here.*

**On the Austro-Hungarian Bank's rate march.** The steps to five and a half (26 October 1912) and the term-structure quotation of 30 November — *Bankzinsfuß 6, Privatdiskont 5½, längere Sichten 6 bis 6¼* — are from the *Neue Freie Presse* money articles. The dating of the six-per-cent step is corroborated by that 30 November quotation; earlier working notes carried it as "[verify]," and it should be checked against the Bank's own published rate history before printing.

**On the German deposit run.** From Lansburgh's *Spargelder und Kriegsflucht* and *Geldklemme* (*Die Bank*, late 1912 and January 1913), read from plain-text transcriptions, not page images. Two figures in those articles — that the Reichsbank's rate already stood at six per cent, and that a single Magdeburg savings bank paid out some three million marks in a week — are the most load-bearing quantities and are marked for verification against the original before publication.

**On the magnitude, stated against interest.** The raw rate deviations for the Balkan winter are large, but so is the 1912 cyclical level; detrended, the money-market signal is modest, of the order of a few tenths of a point, like every other pre-war crisis. The case rests on three things that survive the control — the synchronised timing, the inverted Vienna term structure the London series erased, and the Austrian spread breaking its multi-year decline — not on the size of the raw numbers.

**On the Austrian spread, and what it does not show.** The spread breaks its 1910–12 fall and rises above every control year, the one clean result on the cause-or-cover test. But it builds slowly across the crisis rather than sharply before any decision, consistent with the cumulative cost of a standing mobilisation and not with a market pricing a veto in advance. The chapter claims the first reading and declines the second.

**On causation.** Creditor position and price movement document exposure and a counterparty's condition, not automatic constraint. Finance is causal only where a specific choice can be shown to have bent to it. The formulation here is that finance was the substrate and the lever — that Austria-Hungary spent, at Conrad's vigil, a capacity it then lacked in July 1914 — never that finance *constrained* the Monarchy, which the evidence does not establish.

**On the cost of the mobilisation.** Unestablished. A targeted search of the scholarship returns no clean figure in crowns; the amount, its funding, and whether Biliński recorded the strain in writing lie in Austro-Hungarian budget and delegation proceedings not yet worked through. This is the single most important open source for the chapter, and its central quantitative claim — that the Monarchy spent its war and got none — is at present an inference from the price series and the subsequent dependence on Berlin, not a figure from a ledger.

**On the December 1912 "war council."** A real meeting of the German military leadership on 8 December 1912 (the Kaiser, Moltke, Tirpitz, Müller, Heeringen); its significance is disputed in the scholarship. The specifics used here — Moltke for war sooner, Tirpitz's request for "a postponement of the great fight for one and a half years," the Kiel Canal and Heligoland as the navy's prerequisites, and Röhl's observation on the canal's 1914 completion — follow J. C. G. Röhl, *"War Premeditated? The 'War Council' of 8 December 1912,"* and the German History in Documents and Images edition of the council record. The chapter uses only the least contested feature, that postponement was urged on material (naval, not financial) grounds, and draws no stronger inference than that the men nearest the decision kept finding the machine unready — a habit gone by July 1914.

**On the two Balkan wars.** The claim that the great-power money market ignored the Second Balkan War (summer 1913) rests on the detrended series and on the *Le Temps* liquidation notice of 30 June 1913 (*calme et même indifférence*), which identifies the late-June rate spike as the semester-end settlement rather than the war. The First Balkan War's winter — the Austro-Russian mobilisation scare — is the episode that tightened Vienna and Berlin and ran the German savings banks.

**On the July 1914 underwriting.** That the German assurance to Vienna of 5 July 1914 functioned in part as a financial as well as a strategic guarantee is an interpretation consistent with Austria-Hungary's demonstrated inability to fund a general war alone. It is not a documented financial bargain, and is offered as a reading of the sequence, not an established transaction.
