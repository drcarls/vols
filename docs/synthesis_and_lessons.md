# Synthesis and lessons — from 1911 to Iran 2025

*How the modern arc fits the original war-risk-premium work, what it adds, and what it means for
today. The spine is a single distinction the whole century of evidence keeps drawing:*

> **Markets are poor at anticipating whether a war will happen, and good at pricing its
> consequences once the decision is taken.**

That is the disciplined form of the reader's own instinct — that markets *may be risk-averse and
advocate policy choices, but are also good at pricing things once decisions are taken.* The arc
confirms it and sharpens it into two channels: a **weak** one (anticipation) and a **strong** one
(resolution).

## 1. How the modern cases fit the original (2005) work

The dissertation made three claims about the pre-1914 money market. The modern arc's first job is to
show they were **structural, not a period artifact** — each replicates on cleaner data:

| 2005 finding | Modern replication |
|---|---|
| **Discrimination** — priced *general*-war probability, ignored localized wars and scares | Korea (priced as WWIII, de-priced when limited); Falklands ignored; 2022 & the Gulf Wars priced as *energy* shocks; Berlin '48 discounted; **1939 boom vs 1940 crash** |
| **Modesty** — real premia were small once the cycle is stripped | Pearl Harbor −3.8% *for entry into WWII*; Cuba ~4%; Korea's −13% reversed within weeks |
| **"Cried wolf"** — repeated scares desensitized the market until 1914 | Munich (cried wolf → relief → the real thing); the 1939 boom; the recurring Cold War scares |

Then it **extends** the framework in four directions the sparse 1914 data could not reach:

1. **The un-priceability ceiling** — Cuba '62 / Berlin '61: you cannot price a catastrophe you will
   not survive to collect on. The top of the discrimination ladder, with no pre-nuclear analog.
2. **Instrument migration** — the risk sits wherever the exposure is: sovereign bonds (1905, the
   *fonds russes*), the exchange/reserves (1911, Suez), **oil** (the Gulf Wars, Iran 2025, 2022).
3. **Finance as a weapon** — the freeze lineage (1941 / 1956 / 2022): the 1914 work read finance as
   a *barometer*; the modern arc adds finance as an *instrument of statecraft*, with contingent
   effects (provoked war / forced a retreat / punished without halting).
4. **Dynamic identification** — Korea reprices up and down on one series; Rigobon–Sack measure the
   2003 oil "war factor" formally (the same heteroskedasticity ID this project uses for 1911).

**The book in one line:** a finding born in 1914 archives, validated across a century, extended into
a general theory of how markets price war.

## 2. The two channels — the heart of the synthesis

**Anticipation (weak).** Asking the market *"will they go to war?"* is asking it to price a decision
no one has made. It does this badly:
- It **cries wolf** (Munich 1938; the pre-1914 scares) — rationally, since most scares fizzle.
- It prices the **wrong sign** (1939: it *rallied* 21% on the outbreak of WWII — the war it could
  profit from).
- It is **blindsided by surprises** (Pearl Harbor; the fall of France in eleven days; July 1914's
  flat tape before Sarajevo).
- At the limit it **cannot price** the catastrophe at all (Cuba '62).

The "risk-averse advocacy" the reader names lives in this channel. It is **real but mild, selective,
and often overridden** — the freeze/weapon cases are finance deployed *against* banker caution. So
markets are mediocre war *forecasters*, and Kirshner over-reads this channel as decisive.

**Resolution (strong).** The instant a decision is taken and the uncertainty structure collapses,
the market reprices **fast and accurately**:
- The oil premium vanishing the night **Desert Storm** begins (−30% in a day).
- The **Munich** relief rally; the **Inchon** recovery; the **2003** invasion rally.
- The fall of France priced correctly (−29%) once the blitzkrieg was undeniable.
- **Iran 2025** (below) — the cleanest recent example.

This is the market's genuine talent: not predicting the decision, but **pricing its consequences
once it's made.** The dissertation's title — *"cried wolf once too often"* — is precisely the
anticipation failure; the mature thesis adds that the market was never dumb, it was simply being
asked the wrong question. Put a *taken* decision to it and it excels.

## 3. The live case — Iran, June 2025

The framework applied to the present, and it behaves exactly as the theory predicts.

- **Israel strikes Iran (13 June):** WTI jumps +7%; the run-up peaks **+10% ($75.89, 18 June)** as
  the market prices the **Hormuz-closure tail**. The risk goes entirely into the **barrel** —
  equities barely move.
- **US "Midnight Hammer" strike (21–22 June):** oil **falls** to $69 — *even as the US directly
  enters the war* — because Iran's response was telegraphed and limited (a warned strike on Al
  Udeid; the strait stayed open).
- **Ceasefire (24 June):** WTI **$65.45 — below the pre-crisis level** — and the **S&P at a new
  high.** The entire premium unwound once the supply tail was removed.

Two lessons crystallize here:
- **Oil is the instrument** (test #2): the barrel carried the whole signal; equities made new highs
  throughout. This was priced as a **localized energy event**, not a systemic war (discrimination).
- **Resolution beats anticipation**: the run-up *over-priced* the disruption (oil ended below where
  it started — the anticipation channel was wrong), while the repricing at the decision point was
  right and fast. What moved the market was never *"is the US at war"* but *"will supply be cut"* —
  and the moment that resolved to *no*, the premium was gone.

## 4. Lessons for today

1. **Don't read market calm as a forecast of peace.** A quiet market is not evidence war won't come
   — it may just mean the market can't price an undecided decision (July 1914) or won't survive to
   collect on it (Cuba). Reading calm markets as reassurance about a Taiwan or a wider Middle East
   war is the 1914 error.
2. **Weight consequence-pricing heavily, forecasting lightly.** Markets are a good decision-support
   tool for *"what will this cost once we commit"* and a poor one for *"will they do it."* Iran 2025
   priced the *consequence* (strait open) correctly and the *probability* (the run-up spike) poorly.
3. **Watch the exposed instrument, and watch the resolution, not the level.** Oil for
   energy-transmitted conflict (the Gulf, Iran, 2022); reserves/FX for coercion; the target's
   sovereign bonds for who bears it. The informative moment is the **repricing at the decision**,
   not the ambient calm before.
4. **Finance is a weapon with contingent effects.** Sanctions and reserve freezes are the modern
   coercive instrument; 2022 shows one can *punish without halting*. Don't assume financial pressure
   is decisive — its effect turns on whether the target has a survivable alternative (Japan didn't in
   1941; Britain did in 1956; Russia chose to absorb it in 2022).
5. **On existential risk, market silence means nothing.** You cannot price what you won't survive to
   collect on — so the absence of a premium on nuclear (or arguably climate/AI tail) risk is not
   evidence it is low-probability. Don't outsource existential-risk assessment to prices.

## 5. On Iran specifically (applying the frame forward)

If the question is *"what will the market tell us about an Iran war?"* — the frame says:
- Expect the signal in **oil**, not equities, and expect the market to be **poor at forecasting**
  escalation and **good at pricing** each move once made (as in June 2025).
- A calm oil price is **not** a forecast that nothing will happen — it is the market declining to
  handicap an undecided political choice. The informative event will be the **resolution** of each
  step (a strike, a Hormuz threat carried out or not), where oil will reprice fast.
- The genuine tail is **Hormuz** (a supply catastrophe) — a *survivable, collectable* risk the
  market *can* price, so unlike a nuclear tail it will show up in the barrel when it becomes real,
  and unwind the moment it's removed. June 2025 is the template.
