## Part I — Was Berlin's spot-versus-to-arrive gap unusual in autumn 1911? (No.)

**The claim tested.** That in autumn 1911, at the Agadir climax, Berlin priced *bills to
arrive* dearer than *spot* bills by an amount it did not price in ordinary autumns. If
unusual, the rate "scene" stands on a price; if ordinary, the chapter rests on credit
withdrawal and contemporary belief, with the rates dropped out. "Spot versus to-arrive"
is the *Commercial & Financial Chronicle*'s own wording; the mirrored Neal–Weidenmier
series carries one rate per city and cannot see the spread, so it is read off the
*Chronicle*.

**A single Oct-1 snapshot per autumn suggested 1911 was unique** — the only year Berlin
split its bills (spot 4% / to-arrive 4½%, gap +0.5), with 1910 reading "for both" (0).

**The weekly panel (4–6 obs per autumn) overturns that.** Berlin's gap week by week:
- **1910** — a *war-free* firm autumn (official rate 5%): 17 Sep to-arrive "higher than
  the 4% Bank rate" (≈ +0.6); 1 Oct "for both" (0); **15 Oct 4% spot / 4½% to-arrive
  (+0.5)**. The single snapshot had caught 1910 on its one flat week.
- **1911** (Agadir): a gap runs ~2 Sep → 7 Oct, peaking **+0.5**, gone by November.
- **1912**: 5 Oct "all maturities 3¾%" (0); 19 Oct to-arrive "⅛% above" (+0.125).
- **1908, 1909**: easy money, single rates (0). **1913**: Berlin quoted a single private
  rate all autumn → the gap is *unobservable*, not zero.

**Verdict:** the gap is a **quarter-end forward premium that appears whenever Berlin
money is firm at the turn** — present in war-free 1910 at the same magnitude as 1911 —
**not an Agadir fingerprint.** The earlier "1910 firmer yet flat" claim was a sampling
artifact and is retracted. One honest residue in 1911's favour (not enough to rescue
uniqueness): its gap opens *earlier* (already forming 2 Sep) and comes wrapped in the
Agadir-specific "very high rates bid for foreign assistance" narrative — i.e. in 1911
the quarter-end firmness was itself partly war-driven, but the gap cannot separate
war-firmness from cyclical-firmness.

**Other centres / crises.** Only **London and Berlin** ever split their bills;
Vienna, Paris, Amsterdam, Brussels are single rates throughout (even Vienna at the 1913
Balkan-war climax), so the symmetric "did Vienna split in Austria's crises" test has no
instrument. The **1905** first-Moroccan crisis pre-dates the *Chronicle*'s split-
reporting; the **Bosnian** crisis (1909) was easy money with no gap anywhere.

## Part II — Lansburgh's contemporary German account (autumn 1911)

**"Die Zahlungsbereitschaft der Berliner Banken im Herbst 1911"**, *Die Bank* (Bd 2, pp
1160–1168). Alfred Lansburgh's quantitative post-mortem, from inside Berlin. It
**corroborates the crisis's substance** while **framing it as a quarter-end squeeze** —
exactly what reconciles Part I. Key claims (figures flagged for verification):

- Germany came near a **credit crisis** ("wie nahe wir einer Kreditkrisis gewesen sind");
  a September **deposit run** ("Bankenflucht der Gelder").
- **The French withdrawal** — "die französischen Banken … hoben ihre ausländischen
  Guthaben ab" — the German-side confirmation of the mechanism that stressed Berlin.
- Foreign monies off the eight big banks ≈ **480 M (9½%)**; **Deutsche Bank −224 M
  (12½%)**, which drove it to press the Foreign Office for a calming statement.
- **Reichsbank rationing by mid-September**; rediscounting/lombarding above *even autumn
  1907*.
- **Flight to liquidity** — "nicht einmal deutsche Staatsanleihen wären ohne großen
  Verlust zu realisieren gewesen" (not even German state loans could be sold without
  heavy loss, Sept–Oct).
- **>300 M** withdrawn from the Bourse (call-loan cuts), forcing the September crash.
- **Dependence on foreign short credit** — the banks couldn't place their acceptances
  "angesichts der ablehnenden Haltung des französischen Geldmarktes, des besten
  Abnehmers für deutsche Bankakzepte."
- The frame throughout is the autumn **quarter-end**: "die großen Quartalsansprüche im
  Herbst … der 30. September."
- **Not in Lansburgh:** any explicit "19 September" Reichsbank rate figure (he gives the
  rationing mechanism, not the headline rate); that number must come from a rate series.

## Part III — Lansburgh on the private discount (the term spread, at the quarter-end)

Two 1912 *Die Bank* articles that describe, from inside the Berlin bill market, the same
term spread we measured — and warn against the averaging that hides it.

**"Der Berliner Privatdiskont" (April 1912, Bd 1, 322–328).** On **25 March 1912**, for
the first time in the Bourse's history, the single private-discount quotation broke
down: **short bills (*Schnittwechsel*, 56–70 d) found no demand under 5%, long bills
placed at 4¾%** [verify]. Lansburgh: no accident it appeared "wenige Tage vor einem
**Quartalstermin**," the product of changes that "**seit einem Jahre speziell an den
Terminen**" (for the past year, especially at the settlement dates — i.e. from ~1911)
produced peculiar conditions. The Bourse then moved to **note two rates instead of one**
— conceding the single quotation had masked a real spread.

**"Privatdiskont und Bankdiskont" (later 1912, Bd 2, 733–740).** Opens with a warning
against **averaging**: "die Durchschnittsrechnung … lässt die charakteristischen Höhen
und Tiefen hinter einer nichtssagenden mittleren Linie verschwinden" — study only the
periods where the causes are present, not the spread-compressing quarter-ends. Germany's
private-vs-bank-rate spread ≈ **1%** vs England **0.60%**, France **0.43%**
(Schaer-Reccius, 1901–1910) [verify]; the eligible paper "fast ausschließlich …
Bank-Akzepte."

**Caveat on axes (don't conflate):** Lansburgh's spread is short-vs-long *maturity* (and
on 25 March ran short *above* long); the *Chronicle*'s "spot vs to-arrive" is *delivery
timing* (to-arrive above spot). Related manifestations of the same quarter-end fracture
— present them as parallel, not identical.

## Part IV — Lansburgh on the other crises, and on 1914

His coverage is uneven, and the unevenness ranks the episodes the way the money market does.

- **Bosnian crisis (1908–09): near silence.** No 1908–09 article treats the annexation as
  a financial event (no Bosnien/Annexion/Balkan/Kriegsgefahr; "Kreditkrisen" is a theoretical
  essay). Matches the easy money of Feb–March 1909 — a clean null.
- **Balkan Wars (1912–13): a repeat of 1911.** "Spargelder und Kriegsflucht" (late 1912):
  savers "stürmten zu den Banken und Sparkassen," the Reichsbank — rate *already at 6%*
  [verify] — near raising again; withdrawals worst in East Prussia, the Chancellor wiring a
  calming telegram. "Geldklemme" (Jan 1913): continued squeeze, Reichsbank tightening. Same
  deposit-flight mechanism as autumn 1911.
- **1914 — cyclical calm, then the London freeze.** Early 1914 ("Krisen-Erreger") the worry
  is a *downturn*, not war ("seit etwa einem Jahre geht es… sichtbar bergab") — matching that
  markets didn't price the war in advance. At the outbreak ("Das Geld im Kriege"): "Der
  Weltkrieg, der lange gefürchtete, ist da." Then **"Die Ausschaltung Londons als Clearinghaus
  der Welt"** documents, from Berlin, our NY-control result: claims on London "zum erheblichen
  Teil nicht verwertbar" — the August moratorium (extended), the ban on German/Austrian bills,
  and doubted acceptance-house solvency froze the bill-on-London system worldwide.

## Synthesis — what the chapter can safely say

1. **The crisis was real and severe**, documented from the German side: French
   withdrawal, deposit run, Reichsbank rationing, flight to liquidity (even state bonds
   unsaleable). Lansburgh's own words carry this.
2. **The money-market rates do not isolate a unique war signature.** The spot/to-arrive
   (term) spread is a **quarter-end** feature — present in war-free 1910 — that
   *intensified from 1911*. Lansburgh confirms it from inside Berlin ("especially at the
   settlement dates, for the past year") and warns against the averaging that would hide
   its regime structure.
3. **So the honest spine is credit withdrawal + contemporary belief**, in Lansburgh's
   own words, with the rates used to show Berlin was severely strained at the 1911 turn
   — and Lansburgh doubles as a contemporary methodological ally (don't average across
   settlement regimes).
