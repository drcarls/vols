# NOT THIS YEAR
### Money and the Nine Years Europe Did Not Go to War, 1905–1914

*Draft v6 — populated manuscript. Supersedes v4/v5. Chapter III exists as finished prose in `agadir-essay-magazine.md` (reader version) and `agadir-essay-draft.md` (working version with all qualifications in the body).*

**[TITLE — still unresolved.** *The old subtitle, "Wars Europe Could Not Afford," states the error this book exists to correct. Europe could always afford it. Any title must be about obstruction, timing or capability, never poverty.]*

---

## THE ARGUMENT

By 1905 Europe was ready for its general war. The alliances were signed, the conscript armies raised, the naval race running, the mobilisation timetables printed and revised each year. Everything a great war requires was in place, and it sat there for nine years without being used.

That is what this book is about — not the catastrophe of 1914, which has been explained many times, but the nine years of peace preceding it, which have not.

The war had to be paid for, and paying for it was a step at which things could fail. Not because the money did not exist; it existed, and every treasury knew how to reach it. Because in each crisis between 1905 and 1913 the arrangements could not be made in the time available, or could not be made without a political cost the government would not pay, and somebody had to say so. In July 1914 nobody said so.

**The discipline this book holds to.** A creditor position documents exposure and potential leverage, not automatic constraint. A price movement is a symptom of a counterparty's condition, not proof that finance drove a decision. Finance is causal only where a specific choice can be shown to have bent to it. The formulation is that **finance was the substrate and the lever** — never that finance constrained X, unless a decision demonstrably turned on it.

That is a narrower claim than the one this project began with, and it is the one the evidence supports.

---

## THE FOUR VARIABLES

Asked of each power at each crisis.

**Capability.** Could the state turn national wealth into usable public finance, at the scale and speed required?

**Consent.** Could the government obtain and hold political acceptance of the taxes, loans, inflation and controls a war would demand?

**Liquidity.** Could the treasury and banking system produce spendable money during the crisis itself — not after the harvest, not after a parliamentary fight, not after a foreign loan was negotiated?

**Time.** Would the crisis stay open long enough for a weakness to become visible to ministers and alter the choices in front of them?

**And the distinction the audit must carry throughout: present is not the same as binding.** France's fiscal gap in 1905 was real and entirely irrelevant, because France was never going to fight that year. A constraint binds only when something else would otherwise have permitted the war.

## WHAT THE MARKET SAW

Every week between 1904 and 1914, in thirteen cities from Paris and Berlin to Stockholm, Geneva, Bombay and New York, men set a price for short money. Those prices survive. What they show is not a league table of guilt.

Europe's money markets did not price war country by country. They moved together. Every European centre in the sample loads on a single common factor. Genuine neutrals — Copenhagen, Stockholm — carry premiums as large and as solid as Paris or Vienna. In the Balkan crises neutral Stockholm's premium is significant and Berlin's is not.

**The market was not pricing which country would fight. It was pricing whether the system would hold.**

That is contagion: the probability that a local quarrel runs along the alliances and the bill networks until it becomes general, at which point choosing a side stops mattering. Neutrality was not priced as protection, because it was not protection.

Two things sit on top of that movement. **Germany carried a genuine idiosyncratic excess**, robust when the calculation is re-based away from London — but over the decade as a whole, not crisis by crisis. And **the United States sits outside the structure entirely**, the only city not loading on the European factor, with a negative premium: money came to New York when Europe frightened itself. A country outside the alliance system was where contagion did not reach.

### The crisis ledger

Tested against its own baseline and detrended for the business cycle, the money market sorts the crises by one criterion — whether they could have gone general.

| Crisis | Money-market bite | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Tangier, 1905 | **null** | France's real 1905 exposure was Russian bonds, not Morocco. A bond-market story. |
| Algeciras, 1906 | small, confounded | Pre-1907 stringency. Uncorroborable — the specialist press did not yet exist. |
| Bosnia, 1908–09 | **null** | Money easy; Lansburgh silent. A serious scare that stayed diplomatic. |
| Agadir, 1911 | **modest** | Berlin and Paris co-move about +0.6 detrended at the quarter-end climax — largely the quarter itself. |
| Balkan winter, 1912 | **real** | Great-power mobilisation scare. Austro-Hungarian Bank 5 → 5½ → 6%; deposit runs in Vienna. |
| Tripolitania, 1912 | inconclusive | Cyclically confounded; peaks scattered, not synchronised. |
| Second Balkan War, 1913 | **ignored** | A war, but localised. Paris "calme et indifférence." |
| July 1914 | none, then freeze | Priced at nothing before Sarajevo; then the clearing system froze. |

**Two consequences the manuscript must absorb.**

The crises sort by threat of a *general* war — not by diplomatic heat, and not by whether a war was actually being fought. Europe's money market watched two Balkan wars without flinching and then priced a mobilisation scare that produced no war at all.

And the money-market test measures **rate levels only**. It is silent on quantities. Agadir's evidence is overwhelmingly quantitative — balances withdrawn, Bourse liquidation, central bank rationing, deposit runs — and almost none of that appears in a discount-rate series. Where the money market bit hardest was Vienna in the winter of 1912. Where the story is best documented is Berlin in the autumn of 1911. Those are not the same crisis, and the book must not pretend they are.

## THE INSTRUMENT PROBLEM

The best-known finding about markets and 1914 is that they never saw it coming, established from sovereign bond spreads, which barely register the approach of a general war until the last days of July.

The bond evidence is correct. The conclusion does not follow.

A sovereign bond prices the risk of not being repaid over decades. That risk did not rise, and the market was right that it did not: Britain had suspended convertibility, borrowed enormously through twenty years of war with France, and resumed at par in 1821. Consols had survived Napoleon.

What rose was the price of cash next month, and that lives in a different instrument.

Three things explain the divergence. **Different risks** — war threatened repayment conditionally and liquidity immediately. **Different holders** — the *rente* and consols sat with provincial savers and trustees holding them as savings; bills were rolled every sixty or ninety days by professionals. A bondholder decides once a decade; a discounter decides at breakfast. **And different audiences.** The discount market is where states borrowed short — Treasury bills, *Reichsschatzanweisungen*, *bons du Trésor*. When it tightened, the next issue came dearer or came up short, and that reached a finance minister within days.

The instrument carrying the signal is the channel through which the signal reached the decision. That is the mechanism, not a coincidence of measurement.

And July 1914 confirms it from the other side: when bonds finally moved, their ordering was governed by which asset had a buyer, not by which country would fight.

# THE CHAPTERS

## I. Russia, 1905 — the alliance as an asset

**Morocco is the occasion of this chapter, not its subject.** The crisis everyone remembers from 1905 is the Kaiser at Tangier. The crisis that actually moved French policy that year was happening in Russia, and it reached France through the portfolios of people who had never heard of Agadir.

**Why the usual story does not work.** Tested against the money market, Tangier is null. French rates show nothing; French banks were not squeezed; no French minister was told he could not pay for a war. If this chapter were built on the instrument that carries chapters III and IV, there would be no chapter.

**Where the exposure actually was.** France had bought the Russian alliance with capital — billions of francs of Russian government bonds held by provincial savers, farmers, shopkeepers and schoolteachers who believed they were buying an annuity and had in fact bought a continental army. In 1905 that asset came apart, and the *fonds russes* tracked the Revolution almost day by day: knocked down at Bloody Sunday in January, rallying on the Portsmouth peace in September, crashing again in the December uprising.

Set the two chronologies side by side and the year explains itself.

| | |
|---|---|
| 22 January | Bloody Sunday. Russian bonds fall in Paris. |
| 31 March | The Kaiser lands at Tangier. |
| 27 May | Tsushima. The Russian fleet ceases to exist. |
| 6 June | Delcassé falls. France concedes rather than fight. |
| September | Portsmouth peace. Bonds rally. |
| December | The Moscow uprising. Bonds crash again. |
| Jan–April 1906 | Algeciras. |
| April 1906 | France leads the largest loan in history to Russia. |

France did not concede over Morocco because it could not afford a war. It conceded because its army was convulsed by the *affaire des fiches*, because Britain had made no military commitment — the Entente was colonial, and staff talks began only that December — and because its only continental ally had been destroyed at sea and was in revolution at home.

**But the collapse of that ally was a French financial event as much as a French strategic one**, and it happened to the same people in the same months. A French rentier in the spring of 1905 could watch his savings and his country's security impair together, for the same reason, in the same newspaper column. That is what makes this the right chapter to open a book about money and war: not a treasury saying no, but a nation discovering that its safety was a leveraged position in somebody else's solvency.

**And then France doubled down**, which is what an investor with that exposure does. In April 1906, with Russia at its lowest and the bonds twice broken, France led the largest loan ever raised to that date to the government it needed solvent — and took Russian support at Algeciras as part of the bargain.

That is the book's clearest instance of finance as a *lever*: capital deployed to buy a diplomatic outcome. It is also inferred from timing rather than documented as a quid pro quo, and the chapter must say so.

**Cast.** Rouvier — a banker running the government, who dropped his foreign minister rather than fight. Witte, who needed the money to survive the winter. The provincial holders of Russian paper, who are the reason any of it worked.

### The exhibit

The chapter's central piece of evidence is a chart, and it is the one thing in this book that no reader will have seen before: the price of Russian government bonds on the Paris market, day by day through 1905, with the year's events marked on it.

It should carry three lines and no more.

**The *fonds russes*.** The subject. Falling at Bloody Sunday, sagging through the spring, breaking at Tsushima, recovering on the Portsmouth peace, and broken again in December.

**The French 3% rente.** The control that matters most. If French government paper is steady across the same twelve months while Russian paper is halved, then what happened in 1905 was not a French financial crisis and cannot be described as one. It was a French *portfolio* crisis — which is a different thing and the whole point of the chapter.

**One other foreign sovereign quoted in Paris.** Italian or Spanish, whichever has the cleanest run. This answers the second objection: that Paris was simply marking down foreign risk generally. If the third line sits between the other two, the shock was Russian and specific.

Three lines, twelve months, six event markers. That chart is the chapter.

### The number that makes it human

A price series establishes that Russian bonds fell. It does not establish that anything happened to anybody. The figure that does is the product of two others:

**the fall in price × the amount of Russian debt held in France.**

French holdings of Russian government debt ran into the billions of francs by 1905 and would reach something like ten to twelve billion by 1914, spread across a very large number of small holders — the *petits rentiers* of the provinces, who held Russian paper the way an English clergyman held consols.

Multiply the two and you have what 1905 cost French savers, in francs, before a shot was fired at a Frenchman. As far as I can establish, nobody has put that number in a book. It is the sentence this chapter is built to deliver, and it converts an abstraction — France's alliance was a leveraged position — into a loss suffered by identifiable people in a identifiable year.

**[RESEARCH — the holdings figure is the harder half.** *Estimates of French holdings of Russian debt vary widely and are contested; the standard figures come from the interwar reparations and repudiation literature, which had its own reasons for the numbers it produced. Establish a 1905 figure with a stated source and a stated range, and give the loss as a range rather than a point. A defensible band is worth more here than a precise number that a specialist can dispute.]*

**[RESEARCH — the price series.** *Daily or weekly quotations of Russian 4% and 5% loans on the Paris Bourse across 1905, plus the French 3% rente and one comparator, from* Le Temps *(Gallica, free, full text) or the* Bulletin de la Cote. *The event dates are fixed and public: 22 January, 31 March, 27 May, 6 June, 5 September, December.]*

**Evidence otherwise in hand.** French capital-export figures. The 1906 loan terms and subscription.

**Open.** Whether any document ties the loan to Russian support at Algeciras. French Ministry of Finance records; the Paribas consortium papers; the Russian side in Kokovtsov's and Witte's accounts.

**What this chapter establishes for the rest of the book.** That French capital was an instrument of French policy before it was ever a weapon — and that the men who understood this in 1906 were the same political generation who, five years later, would be accused of turning it on Germany.

## II. Bosnia, 1908–09 — the empty treasury

**The argument.** Russia's ministers did not believe it could finance the prolonged war they expected. The state was recovering from war, revolution and fiscal dislocation, had no direct tax machinery worth the name, and borrowed abroad on terms Paris set.

**The evidence problem, stated up front.** The money market was easy through the Bosnian crisis and Lansburgh is silent on it. Russian spreads were *improving* — 2.01, 2.00, 1.52, 1.24 across 1906–09 — and kept improving through the crisis. That is not the absence of a constraint. It is Russia becoming a better peacetime borrower while remaining unable to fund a general war. **Creditworthiness on existing debt and capacity to mobilise thirty billion roubles are different variables, and only one of them is in a spread.**

This chapter therefore rests on budgets, council records and Kokovtsov's testimony, and the book should explain why — it is the clearest illustration of the instrument problem in reverse.

**Cast.** Kokovtsov. Izvolsky. Aehrenthal.

**The thread forward.** Kokovtsov was dismissed on 30 January 1914. The reasons were mixed — Duma friction, the press, court disfavour — but fiscal conservatism was among them, and the rescript to his successor Bark instructed that the treasury could no longer rest on the ruin of the Tsar's subjects. Within months Russia suspended the spirit monopoly, roughly a quarter to a third of ordinary revenue, in the same weeks it mobilised. **Russia is the one power in the five that opened a new capability gap on the eve of the war rather than closing one.**

Do not argue that prohibition lost Russia the war. Four years of retained vodka revenue covers a fraction of thirty billion roubles of war expenditure. What the decision reveals is that ordinary revenue had stopped mattering, because the war would be short and borrowed.

**Open.** Kokovtsov's memoirs are indispensable and self-serving; test against contemporary budget papers. Verify the revenue share, the timing of the prohibition decree, and whether temperance is actually cited in the dismissal.

## III. Agadir, 1911 — the squeeze

**Status: written.** See `agadir-essay-magazine.md` (reader version, c. 4,500 words) and `agadir-essay-draft.md` (working version).

**The argument as it now stands.** Germany began a confrontation and discovered mid-crisis that its money market could be emptied by the prospect of the war it was threatening. Not brought to its knees — it repaid a hundred and fifty million dollars of French balances before the quarter closed and shipped no gold — but brought to a weak place, which is what produces the response that follows.

**The evidence is quantitative and it is strong.** Four hundred and eighty million marks off the eight great Berlin banks. Deutsche Bank down two hundred and twenty-four million, an eighth of its foreign funding, pressing the Foreign Office for a calming statement. Three hundred million out of the Bourse in call loans. Reichsbank rationing above 1907 levels. A deposit run in the provinces. The mark at its gold point. And the mechanism named by Lansburgh: German banks could not place their acceptances given the refusing attitude of the French money market, the best buyer of German bank acceptances.

**What has been withdrawn.** The spot-versus-to-arrive gap, which resolves into a quarter-end premium rather than a crisis signature. And the claim that the Reichsbank held its rate — it raised a full point on 19 September, and half of Europe followed.

**The unresolved question, honestly framed.** Whether France directed the withdrawal. Four observers in three countries with opposed interests independently named French withdrawals as the operative fact; Caillaux later claimed authorship; the Bank of France's balance sheet shows the money did not accumulate in Paris. The chapter's position: necessity and direction are not alternatives — a government need not manufacture a withdrawal, only be consulted on whose balances get called. Unestablished is the chain of command, not the phenomenon.

**Cast.** Kiderlen and Cambon in a room. Dorizon on a train. Havenstein holding for ten days and then moving. Caillaux at the Place Beauvau. Lansburgh with the balance sheets. Warburg insisting it failed.

## IV. The Balkans, 1912–13 — where the money actually bit

**This chapter is now the book's strongest empirical case and it is the least written.**

Two independent tests point here. On the crisis ledger it is the only pre-war episode rated **real** — a great-power mobilisation scare, with the Austro-Hungarian Bank marching 5 → 5½ → 6% and deposit runs reported in the *Neue Freie Presse*. And on the cause-or-cover control test it is the one clean case: Austria's spread breaks its 1910–12 decline (0.56 → 0.70 → 1.00) above every control year — though it builds slowly across the crisis rather than sharply before the decision.

**The argument.** Austria-Hungary mobilised, spent enormously, fought nobody, and emerged unable to afford the next crisis. Of the five powers it is the only one that never closed the gap the crisis exposed — which is why, in July 1914, the repair had to be performed for it by an ally.

**A methodological point the book should make here.** The *Neue Freie Presse* shows a Viennese term structure that the London-based trade press flattened to "Vienna 6%." The Anglophone record of continental money markets is a compression of the continental record. That is why this project reads Vienna in Vienna, Berlin in Berlin, Paris in Paris.

**Cast.** Biliński. Conrad. And on the German side, the December 1912 council that produced a request for postponement on material grounds.

**Open, and this is the priority.** Austro-Hungarian budget and delegation records: what the mobilisation actually cost and how it was funded. Whether Biliński said in writing what the ledger implies. Whether anything was attempted by way of repair.

## V. July 1914 — the question nobody asked

**The argument.** In every crisis from 1905 to 1913 the money question was asked, and a finance principal answered it. In July 1914 the decision circle was Bethmann, Wilhelm, Jagow, Zimmermann, Moltke. No Reichsbank president. No Treasury secretary.

**And the German financial apparatus did nothing during the quiet weeks.** The published record shows the informal steps beginning only during the late-July panic: the Reichsbank extending the money supply, suspending specie payments on 31 July, the imperial gold reserve of 240 million marks transferred on 2 August. Nothing between 5 and 23 July. The state had knowledge the market lacked for eighteen days and took no financial action, because the men who would have acted had not been told.

**Why the market said nothing.** It had nothing to price. Germany's assurance to Vienna was private, on the fifth. The ultimatum was drafted through the middle of the month in deliberate quiet, timed for after the French president had sailed from St Petersburg, and delivered on the twenty-third with a forty-eight-hour limit whose purpose was to prevent the dispute passing into a conference, as every dispute since 1905 had.

So the market's first opportunity to price the crisis was the ultimatum — and the ultimatum was not a step toward the decision. It was the decision becoming visible.

**Be careful with the timing claim.** Material stress appeared within about a fortnight in the earlier crises, and in the last week of July 1914 it appeared on schedule and with force: consols down nearly six per cent, German bonds five, Austrian six, the Bank of England from three to ten in four days. The brake was not slow. **The argument rests on sequence, not duration** — the assurance of 5 July and the ultimatum of 23 July both precede any possible market reaction — and that rests on dates alone, which is where it should stay.

**And the last-week cross-section measures marketability, not war risk.** Consols fell hardest because they were the most saleable asset on earth. The aggregate fall is the war signal; the ordering is liquidity.

**Cast.** Bethmann. Berchtold. Havenstein, not consulted.

**Open.** Whether anything in the Reichsbank or Treasury files shows action in the 5–23 July window. Zilch; the Reichsbank *Verwaltungsbericht* for 1914; BArch R 2 and R 2501.

## THE ENGINE: "NEVER AGAIN"

Each crisis exposed one power's gap, and the years afterwards were spent closing it.

| Crisis | Exposed | Revealed | Built afterwards |
|---|---|---|---|
| Morocco I, 1905–06 | France | No modern extraction; Russian weakness transmitted into French portfolios | Caillaux's campaign for an income tax; French capital rebuilding Russia |
| Bosnia, 1908–09 | Russia | Could not fund the war its ministers expected | Rearmament and strategic railways, financed from Paris |
| Agadir, 1911 | Germany | Rich but illiquid; dependent on foreign short credit | *Wehrbeitrag* 1913; Juliusturm doubled; gold coin drawn in; twice-monthly bank reporting; the rate cartel; Havenstein's liquidity reform, **completed July 1914** |
| Balkans, 1912–13 | Austria-Hungary | Capacity spent on a mobilisation that fought nobody | **Nothing established** |
| July 1914 | Austria and Germany | Vienna's gap, covered by German assurance | War before the arrangement was tested |

**The last two rows are the argument.** Four powers found their weakness and worked at it. One could not, and the repair was performed for it on credit, a fortnight before the ultimatum.

**[EVIDENTIARY LIMIT.** *Do not write "underwriting" until it is documented. Whether the assurance of 5 July amounted to a specific financial commitment, or was political and strategic support this book is reading financially, must be demonstrated. It is currently an interpretation and the most load-bearing one in the book.]*

**[CAUTION.** *Prudential reserve-building and preparation for aggression are identical on a balance sheet. The test is whether ministers themselves connected the vulnerability they experienced with the measures they adopted — documentary, not quantitative. On the German side one contemporary sentence does most of the work: a New York weekly in the first week of August 1914 noting the Reichsbank had added upwards of a hundred million dollars of gold since the Morocco incident.]*

## THE FRENCH THREAD

In 1905 France had no general income tax. It funded itself on levies inherited from the Revolution and on the *rente* — perpetual bonds held by farmers, shopkeepers and schoolteachers as savings and as patriotic habit. A wealthy country with no modern instrument for reaching its own wealth quickly.

Joseph Caillaux spent the next decade trying to change that. The Senate called the assessment machinery an inquisition.

The law passed on 15 July 1914.

Caillaux was not in office to see it. He had resigned after his wife walked into the offices of *Le Figaro* and shot the editor. Her trial filled the French press through the last weeks of peace; she was acquitted on 28 July, the day Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia.

France had authorised the instrument it lacked. It had not built the administration or collected a franc.

**And one line runs alongside it.** Britain had funded wars on parliamentary credit since the 1690s, and by 1912–13 was spending about three per cent of national income on defence with debt falling from roughly a quarter of national income to under a fifth — deleveraging while Germany's budget committed to increases through 1917. France's income tax: July 1914. Germany's war profits tax: 1916. Russia's income tax: 1916. **Every continental power built its extraction machinery during or immediately before the war it needed it for. Nobody was ready.**

## RESEARCH SCHEDULE

### Priorities, revised

1. **Austria-Hungary, 1912–13.** The strongest empirical case and the least developed chapter. Delegation proceedings on the common budget; what the mobilisation cost and how it was funded; the *Neue Freie Presse* through the winter.
2. **Deutsche Bank Historisches Institut, Frankfurt.** Whether the six-million-franc credit existed; how the bank described foreign-credit conditions; **and the approach to the Foreign Office**, which is now the single most load-bearing sentence in Chapter III and rests on one line of transcribed text.
3. **Nantes** — the Berlin embassy's own files, for Dorizon's errand and for what Paris asked Cambon to report.
4. **La Courneuve** — whether the Quai d'Orsay encouraged the withdrawals or knew of them.
5. **Zilch, and the Reichsbank *Verwaltungsberichte* (BArch R 2501)** — the 5–23 July 1914 question, and the internal reasoning behind the 19 September 1911 rate rise.
6. **Russian material for Chapter II** — budget papers and council records against Kokovtsov's memoirs.

### Sources and discipline

Neal–Weidenmier weekly rates. *Commercial and Financial Chronicle* (FRASER). Lansburgh's *Die Bank* (Zenodo 5115892). *Neue Freie Presse* (ANNO). *Le Temps* (Gallica). Ellison & Scott for market-value UK debt. Correlates of War for 1913 trade shares.

Every transcribed figure is OCR-grade. Fractional rates come off page images; OCR locates, it never authorises a number. Where tables carry internal arithmetic, use row and column sums as validity checks. Every quotation checked against the image and recorded with issue, date and page.

### What would weaken or falsify the argument

- Financial stress consistently *following* the key diplomatic decisions.
- Treasury and central-bank officials not communicating material constraints to political leaders.
- Official records failing to connect financial weakness to recommendations for restraint.
- Post-crisis repairs unrelated to the vulnerabilities the crises revealed.
- Governments in July 1914 understanding the constraints and consciously overriding them well before the ultimatum.

*The last would not make finance irrelevant. It would make this a book about the deliberate overriding of financial restraint rather than a warning that arrived too late — a different book, and still a good one.*

### And a limit to state once, in the text

Four crises; roughly four usable control years, each carrying its own disturbance; strong secular trends in Russian and Austrian spreads. **This sample cannot adjudicate cause versus cover in either direction, however it is sliced.** Say so once, let the archives carry the causal claim, and no reviewer can accuse the book of overreach or of evasion.

# AFTERWORD — THE METHOD BEYOND 1914

*A closing part for the book, in the book's voice and under the book's discipline. It does not belong to the 1905–1914 argument and must not be folded into it; it is the account of what the instrument built for those nine years sees when it is pointed at the rest of the century — and at the week this book went to press. Charted modern series are reproducible (daily S&P via Yahoo `^GSPC` back to 1928; FRED for oil and post-1957 equities); documentary and prediction-market figures are cited, point-in-time, and carry [VERIFY]. Full workings in the repository: `docs/synthesis_and_lessons.md`, `docs/modern_*.md`, and `docs/research_agenda.md`.*

---

The instrument this book built to read nine years of European peace was not built for them. It was built for the general problem the nine years happen to pose in their purest form: how a market prices the risk of a war, and what that pricing does and does not tell a government. Pointed forward, the same instrument keeps working, and it keeps returning the same three findings — with one addition the pre-1914 world could not supply, and one limit it could not reach.

A word on register first, because it is a real seam and not a rhetorical one. This book is about *non-events* — the wars that did not happen, the constraint that is a level and not a movement, the silence of an easy money market as the clearest evidence there is. The cases that follow are mostly about *events* — wars that happened, and were priced as they happened. The method travels; the subject shifts. What survives the shift is the method's four questions and its one discipline, and that is the claim of this afterword: not that 1914 and 1991 are the same, but that the same audit reads them both.

### Discrimination, across a century

The crisis ledger of "What the Market Saw" sorted the pre-war crises by a single criterion — whether they could have gone *general*. The market ignored two Balkan wars and priced a mobilisation scare that produced no war at all. That is not a quirk of thin Edwardian data. It is the rule, and the twentieth century states it in daily prices.

| Rung | Verdict | The cases |
|---|---|---|
| Localised war | **ignored** | Second Balkan 1913; Falklands 1982 — real wars the great markets discounted |
| General, judged unlikely | **discounted** | Berlin Airlift 1948 — the market rose *through* the war scare |
| General and survivable | **priced** | Korea 1950; the Gulf Wars; 2022 — priced, and dynamically |
| Existential | **un-priceable** | Cuba 1962; Berlin 1961 — the limit (below) |

Korea is the case the pre-war data was too thin to show: a single war repriced *up and down* on one continuous series as its probability of going general rose and fell. It broke roughly thirteen per cent on the June 1950 invasion — feared, in that summer, as the opening of a third world war — recovered its whole loss once Inchon made the war look contained, broke again when China's entry revived the general-war fear, and made new highs once the thing settled into a limited war. The market was never pricing "a war." It was pricing the probability of *the* war, exactly as Stockholm and Copenhagen were in 1912. And the outbreak of the Second World War makes the same point from the opposite side: the American market *rose* twenty-one per cent on the invasion of Poland — the war a neutral supplier could profit from — and did not crash until the fall of France eight months later turned a distant boom into a systemic threat. Same war; repriced when its character changed.

### Anticipation and resolution — and why Chapter V is the ur-case

The century sharpens the book's most important qualification into a general finding, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the sentence a reader will carry out of the whole project:

> Markets are poor at anticipating whether a war will happen, and good at pricing its consequences once the decision is taken.

Chapter V is the origin of this, not an application of it. The July 1914 money market said nothing before the ultimatum because it had nothing to price — the decision was private on the fifth and invisible until the twenty-third — and then it seized within the week. That is the two-channel finding in its first and clearest form: a **weak anticipation channel** (the market cannot price a decision that has not been made, and discounts the war-talk that has cried wolf before) and a **strong resolution channel** (once the decision becomes visible, the repricing is fast, large, and correct). Everything forward confirms it. Munich cried wolf and then rallied on the accord. Pearl Harbor, a genuine surprise, drew only a modest first-day fall because a surprise offers nothing to anticipate and the war was already half in the price. The oil premium of 1990 collapsed thirty per cent in a single day when Desert Storm began — not because the war ended but because the *uncertainty* did, and a central bank said as much in its own bulletin that quarter. The market's talent is not prophecy. It is the pricing of a taken decision.

This is also the disciplined answer to the objection that this book merely restates that markets are war-averse. The anticipation channel is where war-aversion would live, and it is the market's *weak* channel — mild, selective, and, in the cases that follow, repeatedly overridden. What the market does well is not advocacy. It is arithmetic on a decision already made.

### The instrument problem, confirmed

"The Instrument Problem" argued that the signal lives in whichever instrument is exposed, and that the instrument carrying the signal is the channel through which the signal reaches the decision. The century is a catalogue of the same mechanism with the instrument changed. In 1905 the exposure was a sovereign bond and the signal was in the *fonds russes*. In 1911 and at Suez it was the exchange and the reserve. In the Gulf, in 2022, and over Iran it is **oil** — the modern discount market, the place where the risk of the *next barrel* is priced the way the risk of the next month's bills once was. The lesson is unchanged: read the exposed instrument, and read the resolution, not the ambient level. The economist Roberto Rigobon and the central banker Brian Sack measured exactly this for the 2003 Iraq war — a "war-risk factor" that raised oil and lowered equities — by the same heteroskedasticity identification this project uses to read Berlin in 1911. That two independent efforts reach the same estimator across a century is the strongest evidence that the method is about structure, not period.

### The one addition — finance as a weapon

Here the modern record adds something the book's discipline was right to withhold from 1905–1914. The pre-war formulation was that finance was *the substrate and the lever* — never the constraint unless a decision demonstrably turned on it. That discipline holds for the nine years. But the twentieth-century state learned to wield finance as an outright *weapon*, and the evidence for it is documentary in a way the pre-war squeeze never was. The lineage is three freezes: the American freeze of Japanese assets in 1941, which cornered Japan toward war; the American denial of sterling support at Suez in 1956, which forced a great power to abort a war it was winning; and the freezing of Russian reserves in 2022, which punished without halting. One weapon, three outcomes — provoked, coerced, punished — and the effect turns on whether the target has a survivable alternative. The discipline of Chapter III applies unchanged: the *effect* is documented in every case; the *decisiveness* must be argued, never assumed. This is the friendly amendment the modern record makes to the book, and the one place the story grows a face the pre-war chapters do not show.

### The limit — the risk that cannot be priced

And a limit, at the top of the ladder, that closes the method honestly. In October 1962 the American market fell only a few per cent through the most dangerous fortnight in human history, and its low came *before* the most dangerous days; it rose on the morning the blockade was enforced. The reason is not that the market judged the risk small. It is that there is no state of the world in which selling paid off — a claim on a future that, in the catastrophic branch, does not exist. **You cannot price a catastrophe you will not survive to collect on.** The discrimination test has a ceiling, and existential risk sits above it, unpriced not because it is improbable but because the instrument cannot represent it. The absence of a premium is not the absence of a danger. That is the last thing the method has to say, and it is a warning, not a null.

## NOT THIS YEAR, AGAIN — a closing note written to the week

This book takes its title from the answer the money question received in every crisis from 1905 to 1913: not this year. It is being finished in a year when the question is live again, and the honest thing is to let the method speak to it under its own discipline rather than to end on the safety of the past.

As these pages go to press, in the late summer of 2026, there is a war between Iran on one side and the United States and Israel on the other, a fragile and repeatedly violated ceasefire, and a Strait of Hormuz that has already been closed once this year and may be again. When it was closed, in the spring, oil ran from the high fifties to a hundred and fourteen dollars and held above a hundred for two months — the tail *materialising*, where a year earlier, in June 2025, the same strait was only threatened and oil spiked and fell back below its starting point within days. The instrument behaved exactly as the method says it should: the size and the *duration* of the move discriminated a real closure from a scare, and equities, through all of it, treated a Middle Eastern war as an energy shock and made new highs — pricing whether the *system* would hold, not who would fight, precisely as Europe's money market did in 1912.

Two things about this moment the pre-war economist could not have had. The first is that the *anticipation* channel — the market's weak one, the one that failed in July 1914 — is for the first time directly observable. Prediction markets now price the decision itself: as this is written, the crowd puts American forces entering Iran by year's end near ninety per cent and normal traffic through Hormuz by September at under half, with tens of millions of dollars staked. For the first time one can *decompose* the oil price — the probability of disruption read off the prediction market, the size read off the futures curve — and in the spring the odds of a reopening moved first and the oil price followed. The century-old blind spot has an instrument pointed at it. The second thing is a caution the book has earned the right to give: that instrument is thin and manipulable, the prediction market can cry wolf as loudly as any rumour ever did, and the resolution channel still dominates — the clean repricing is at the actual closure or the durable ceasefire, not in today's oscillating price or today's odds.

So the method declines, on principle, to tell you how it ends. What it will say is structural and it is enough. The tradeable tail is Hormuz; it is survivable and collectable, so unlike the nuclear tail the market *can* price it, and it will hold a large premium only while the strait is actually shut. A calm oil price is not a forecast of peace — it is the market declining to handicap a decision no one has yet made, which is the exact error a generation of observers made in the summer of 1914. The book's whole argument is that the money question is a step at which things can fail, and that somebody has to be in the room to ask it. The market cannot ask it for you. It can only price the answer once you have given one.

Not this year, the century keeps saying, until the year it doesn't.
