# Lansburgh on the *other* crises, and on 1914

What Alfred Lansburgh (*Die Bank*) wrote about the pre-war crises beyond Agadir — the
Bosnian annexation (1908–09), the Balkan Wars (1912–13) — and about 1914. His coverage
is strikingly uneven, and the unevenness is itself evidence: he ranks the episodes the
same way the money market does.

Provenance: Zenodo 5115892 (CC-BY-4.0); Lansburgh d. 1937 → PD 2008. Plain text, not
page images — **verify every figure against the original before publication.**

## Bosnian crisis (1908–09): near silence

Lansburgh's 1908–09 money-market writing **does not treat the annexation crisis as a
financial event.** No article that year mentions Bosnien / Annexion / Balkan /
Kriegsgefahr, and "Kreditkrisen" (1909, Bd 1, 1–10) — the title one would expect to
engage it — is a *theoretical* essay (it opens on the socialist theory of "anarchic
production" and cartelisation), not crisis reporting. This matches the rate record: the
money market was easy in Feb–March 1909, so the war scare left no money-market trace. A
clean null — the 1908–09 crisis was diplomatic, not a German *money-market* crisis.

## Balkan Wars (1912–13): a repeat of 1911, on the eastern border

**"Spargelder und Kriegsflucht"** (Bd 2, 1147–1153, late 1912) is the Balkan-winter
analogue of the autumn-1911 post-mortem — the *same* deposit-run mechanism, geographically
concentrated toward Russia:

> *"Zahlreiche Sparer waren zu den Banken und Sparkassen gestürmt um einen Teil ihres
> Guthabens abzuheben oder zu kündigen… Eine Zeitlang hatte es sogar den Anschein als
> wenn die Reichsbank den Ansturm auf ihre Diskontkassen… mit einer weiteren Erhöhung
> ihres erst vor kurzem auf 6% hinaufgesetzten Diskontsatzes würde begegnen müssen."*

Savers stormed the banks and savings banks; the Reichsbank — its rate **already at 6%**
[VERIFY] — nearly had to raise again. Withdrawals were largest in **East Prussia**, so
the **Chancellor sent a calming telegram** to its Oberpräsident and the press opened a
campaign against hoarding; the Magdeburg savings bank alone paid out **~3 M in one week**
[VERIFY]. Then **"Geldklemme"** (Bd 1, 25–30, Jan 1913) records the continued squeeze:
the Reichsbank president had warned for two years about excess credit and thin cash
reserves; his sharpest steps were to stop discounting repeatedly-prolonged credit bills
and to tighten Lombard terms. So autumn 1911 was **not unique in kind** — the Balkan
winter produced the same war-fear deposit flight and Reichsbank strain (and explains why
the *Chronicle* had Berlin at 6% that December, "for both spot and to arrive").

## 1914: cyclical calm before the war, then the London freeze

**Before the war — the worry was the business cycle, not war.**
- **"Krisen-Erreger"** (Bd 1, 1–16, Jan 1914): *"Seit etwa einem Jahre geht es mit der
  Konjunktur in Deutschland sichtbar bergab."* A year-long downturn — thinning order
  books, unusually high bankruptcies; *"die Zeit in der wir leben trägt viele wenn auch
  nicht alle charakteristischen Züge der Krisis."*
- **"Die Reichsbank und der sogenannte Geldmarkt"** (Bd 1, 113–120): the "critical days"
  of industry were the consequence of an *"maßlosen… Überfütterung mit Kredit"* — the
  Reichsbank partly to blame for accustoming the banks to run without cash reserves.

  This is exactly the backdrop our rate work found: markets **did not price the war in
  advance** (June-1914 deviations at or below seasonal norm). Lansburgh in early 1914 is
  worried about a *recession*, not a war.

**The outbreak.**
- **"Das Geld im Kriege"** (Bd 2, 727–738): *"Der Weltkrieg, der lange gefürchtete, ist
  da."* Straight to war finance — where does the state find the billions; uncovered notes
  vs loans. (Continued in **"Die Kriegskosten-Deckung und ihre Quellen,"** two parts, and
  **"Gedanken über die Milliardenanleihe."**)

**The London freeze — our NY-control / contagion result, documented from Berlin.**
- **"Die Ausschaltung Londons als Clearinghaus der Welt"** (Bd 2, 903–920):

> *"Die Tatsache dass der größte Geldmarkt der Welt seit zwei Monaten in einen Krieg
> verwickelt ist… hat die ganze Güter herstellende und Handel treibende Kulturwelt in
> eine seltsame Lage gebracht. Forderungen welche die Länder an den Londoner Geldmarkt
> haben sind zum erheblichen Teil nicht verwertbar."*

  Claims on London became largely unrealizable, for three reasons he lists: (1) the
  **moratorium** issued early August and repeatedly extended, which put the timely
  redemption of English acceptances in doubt; (2) the ban on honouring any bill arising
  from business with **Germany or Austria**; (3) English acceptance/trading houses so
  damaged that their signature was refused abroad. This is the freeze of the
  bill-on-London system that transmitted the crisis worldwide — the mechanism behind New
  York's sign-flip in our data, seen from the German side.

**After.**
- **"Der Abbau der Berliner Börsenkurse"** (Bd 2, 1019–1030): *"Mehr als ein Vierteljahr
  ist seit dem Ausbruch des großen Krieges verflossen und noch immer sind die maßgebenden
  Effektenbörsen der Welt geschlossen"* — the exchanges stayed shut for fear that the
  first to reopen would be flooded with international paper and drag all prices down.
- **"Wirtschaftliche Kriegsbereitschaft"** (Bd 2, 819–828): skeptical of the glib
  post-crisis optimism that claims an economy has proved "surprisingly resilient."

## The through-line for the chapter

Lansburgh's commentary ranks the pre-war episodes exactly as the money market does:
**Bosnia 1908–09 → nothing** (easy money, no coverage); **Agadir 1911 and the Balkan
winter 1912 → real deposit-flight / Reichsbank crises** of the same type; **1914 → a
cyclical downturn that was not read as war until London froze in August.** It reinforces
that the war-risk signal lived in **deposit flight and Reichsbank strain**, not in a
unique rate spread — and it gives the July-1914 chapter a German-side witness to the
London freeze.
