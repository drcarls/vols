## A. AGADIR — "The Squeeze" (Chapter III)

### A1. The man who carried the alarm — Arthur von Gwinner (research; honest inference)

The essay says the Deutsche Bank pressed the Foreign Office for a calming statement. The sourced record names the **institution, not a person**: Lansburgh writes only "the Deutsche Bank," and *Le Temps* (30 Sep 1911) refers to *"les directeurs des grandes banques de Berlin"* receiving the government's assurances. The likely individual is **Arthur von Gwinner**, Spokesman of Deutsche Bank's Management Board 1910–1919, responsible for its international operations and known inside the bank as "the bank's diplomat" (Deutsche Bank Historical Association).

- **Paste-safe form:** *"the Deutsche Bank — whose board spokesman and 'diplomat' was Arthur von Gwinner — pressed Kiderlen's Foreign Office for reassurance."*
- **Do not** write "Gwinner went to Kiderlen" as fact. This is role-based inference, not a documented act; no source places him personally at the Wilhelmstrasse that week. Pinning the person would need a Deutsche Bank corporate history or the Auswärtiges-Amt / *Große Politik* files for late September 1911.

### A2. Lansburgh in the original German (for the notes / scholarly grounding)

The chapter renders Lansburgh in English. The originals, for a footnote or a bilingual pull-quote:

> *"…angesichts der ablehnenden Haltung des französischen Geldmarktes, des besten Abnehmers für deutsche Bankakzepte."*
> — the refusing attitude of the French money market, the best buyer of German bank acceptances.

> *"In den kritischen Tagen des September und Oktober wären nicht einmal deutsche Staatsanleihen ohne grossen Verlust zu realisieren gewesen."*
> — in the critical days of September and October, not even German state loans could have been sold without heavy loss.

> *"…wie nahe wir einer Kreditkrisis gewesen sind."*
> — how near we came to a credit crisis.

Source: A. Lansburgh, *"Die Zahlungsbereitschaft der Berliner Banken im Herbst 1911,"* *Die Bank* Bd 2, pp. 1160–1168. Zenodo 5115892 (CC-BY-4.0). `[VERIFY vs page image]`

### A3. Corroboration for the term-structure note — the 25 March 1912 break

The chapter's NOTES withdraw the spot-vs-to-arrive gap as a crisis signature and keep it as a quarter-end premium. Lansburgh, from inside the Berlin bill market, documents the single private-discount quotation breaking for the first time at a quarter-end:

> *"…am 25. März trat zum ersten Male der Fall ein dass eine Einigung überhaupt nicht zu erzielen war. Für Schnittwechsel war keine Nachfrage unter 5% da, während lange Wechsel zu 4¾% unterzubringen waren."*
> — on 25 March, for the first time, no single rate could be agreed: short bills found no demand under 5%, long bills placed at 4¾%.

He dates the phenomenon "for the past year, especially at the settlement dates" — i.e. from about spring 1911, the Agadir-era strain. This is the contemporary basis for the chapter's own statistical caution.
Source: A. Lansburgh, *"Der Berliner Privatdiskont,"* *Die Bank* (April 1912), Bd 1, pp. 322–328. `[VERIFY]`

### A4. Discipline check — what NOT to add

A web sweep for contemporary coverage of the September 1911 Berlin panic returns mainly two claims: that the Berlin bourse fell 30 per cent in a single day, and that the Reichsbank lost a fifth of its gold reserves in a month. Both circulate without primary citation, and the chapter's NOTES already mark them **unused** for exactly that reason. Keep them out until a primary source is found. (The academic literature on the 1911 Reichsbank — e.g. the NBER chapter on the German central bank and the rules of the game — is the place to look for verified figures; the popular sources are not.)

## B. THE BALKANS — "Spent" (Chapter IV)

### B1. The December 1912 War Council — the "material grounds," now sourced

The chapter's §VI says postponement was urged on material grounds. The record supports a precise version. At the German Imperial War Council of **8 December 1912** (present: Wilhelm II, Tirpitz, Admiral von Müller, Moltke, Admiral von Heeringen), Moltke argued for war "the sooner the better," while **Tirpitz asked for "a postponement of the great fight for one and a half years,"** because the navy was not ready for a war that included Britain. His stated prerequisites were **the widening of the Kiel Canal and the U-boat base at Heligoland.** John Röhl notes the coincidence that the canal's widening was due for completion in the summer of 1914 — while adding that the report shows no agreement on a war in 1914.

- **Use it as:** evidence that the men nearest the decision kept finding the machine unready. **Caveat, in the notes:** this is the *German* council, its significance is disputed in the scholarship, and the "material" at issue was naval and engineering, not financial. Do not read it as a financial postponement.
- Sources: J. C. G. Röhl, *"War Premeditated? The 'War Council' of 8 December 1912"*; German History in Documents and Images (GHDI), "The 'War Council' (December 1912)."

### B2. Conrad reinstated, December 1912

Conrad von Hötzendorf was **reinstated as Chief of the General Staff in December 1912**, as a major war seemed imminent; he continued to advocate preventive war against Serbia and Italy, and his staff drew up separate war plans — Case I (Italy), Case R (Russia), Case B (Serbia), and R+B. A concrete anchor for the chapter's "Conrad's vigil."
Source: *1914-1918-Online*, "Pre-war Military Planning (Austria-Hungary)"; Britannica, "Austria — Conflict with Serbia."

### B3. A token of the scale — the Mobilization Cross 1912/13

Austria-Hungary struck a decoration, the **Mobilization Cross 1912/13** (*Mobilisierungskreuz*), awarded to members of the armed forces called up in the 1912 mobilization who served at least four weeks. A tangible marker that the standing mobilization of the Balkan winter was large and long enough to warrant its own medal — useful colour for the "vigil that cost a war and fought nobody."
Source: "Mobilization Cross 1912/13" (standard reference). `[VERIFY exact terms]`
