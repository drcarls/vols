# THE STRAIT
### Hormuz, 2025–2026

*Modern companion to the earlier chapters, and the one case in the book that is not closed. The working evidence — the FRED oil and equity series, the dated prediction-market figures, and every source — is in `modern_iran_2026_prediction_markets.md`. Prediction-market numbers are point-in-time and flagged for verification; the situation was still live as this was written.*

---

The book has spent a century arguing that markets price the consequences of a war well and forecast the decision to fight it badly, and that the signal lives in a specific exposed instrument. Iran gave both halves of that claim a controlled test, twice in two years, on the same instrument: the price of oil, moving with the Strait of Hormuz. In 2025 the strait was threatened and stayed open. In 2026 it was actually shut. The oil price treated the two as different worlds, which is exactly what the framework predicts — and along the way a new instrument appeared that reads the half of the problem the old ones never could.

---

## I.

June 2025 was the threat. Israel struck Iran on the thirteenth, and WTI rose about seven per cent; the run-up peaked ten per cent higher, at $75.89 on the eighteenth, as the market priced the tail everyone was watching — a closure of Hormuz, through which something like a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes. The risk went almost entirely into the barrel; equities barely moved.

Then the decision came, and it went the other way. On the night of the twenty-first the United States struck the Iranian nuclear sites directly, entering the war — and oil *fell*, to $69, even as the superpower joined the fight. It fell because Iran's response was telegraphed and limited: a warned strike on the American base at Al Udeid, and no move to close the strait. The thing the price had feared did not happen, and the price said so at once. The ceasefire on the twenty-fourth took WTI to $65.45, below where it had started, with the S&P at a new high. The entire premium had unwound the moment the supply tail was removed.

The lesson is the book's, stated on live data. Oil was the instrument — the barrel carried the whole signal while equities made new highs throughout. And resolution beat anticipation: the run-up over-priced the disruption, so much that oil ended the crisis below where it began, while the repricing at each taken decision — the US strike, the ceasefire — was fast and right. What moved the market was never "is the United States at war" but "will the supply be cut," and the moment that resolved to no, the premium was gone.

---

## II.

2026 was the tail itself. A US–Israel–Iran war broke out in late February, and this time the Strait of Hormuz was virtually shut, roughly eight million barrels a day removed. Oil behaved nothing like the year before: WTI surged about fifty-two per cent in March, to a peak near $114, and held above $100 through April and May — not the days-long spike-and-collapse of June 2025, but a sustained premium, because the disruption was real and continuing.

The same instrument gave two magnitudes because the tail was real the second time and not the first — the discrimination test operating on the oil rung, in real time. Equities discriminated too. The S&P dipped to around 6575 in April and recovered to new highs near 7600 by June, with a Middle East war under way and the strait shut, because it was still priced as an energy and regional shock rather than a systemic one. The 2022 finding, replayed: a war that stays in the barrel and off the alliances is, to the equity index, a commodity event.

---

## III.

The new development is the one that ties this chapter to the trading work, and it is a genuine change in how war risk can be read. Historically the market's weak channel was anticipation: you could never cleanly read "will they go to war" off an asset price, because oil blends the *probability* of a disruption with its *size*, and equities blend war with the business cycle. In 2026, prediction markets priced the decision itself, in the open, with real money. Polymarket carried "US forces enter Iran by 31 December" at about ninety per cent, an outright "Iran invasion" market at thirty-six per cent on the day Iran closed the strait, and "Kharg Island falls by 30 June" — Iran's main oil terminal — at thirty-one. Kalshi ran "normal Hormuz traffic by 1 September" near forty-six per cent.

For the first time the oil premium can be decomposed. Its size is `P(disruption) × size of disruption`, and now the two factors are separately visible: read the probability off Polymarket and Kalshi, and the size off the oil futures curve. The pre-1914 analyst, and the 1991 or even the 2022 one, had only the product. And the two began to move in sequence: contemporaneous reporting was explicit that the prediction markets priced Hormuz reopening after the US strike pause, "signaling lower oil prices" — the odds moved first and oil followed. This is the raw material the geopolitical sleeve reads: the decision-odds on one screen, the priced consequence on another, and the gap between them the thing to trade.

---

## IV.

The book's own tests turn on the new instrument as readily as on the old ones. Prediction markets discriminate more finely than oil can — among US entry, invasion, days-of-closure, and Kharg falling — pricing the structure of the risk and not just its magnitude. Whether they also cry wolf is the open question: the early 2026 evidence is that they were reasonably calibrated and fast, with the invasion odds rising as the strait closed and the reopening odds rising on the strike pause and leading oil. But they are thin and manipulable, and Polymarket has had known manipulation episodes, so the cried-wolf discipline the book applied to the money market now attaches to the prediction market too — a moving odds line is a lead, not a proof, and a large bet can be a signal or a spoof.

Resolution still dominates. Even with the odds observable, the cleanest and most accurate repricing is still at the taken decision — the strait actually closing, the strike pause — not the forecast. Prediction markets improve the anticipation read; they do not overturn the finding that a market prices a decision best once it has been made. What is new is reflexivity: oil traders watch the odds and bettors watch oil, a feedback loop that did not exist in 1914, and one that can sharpen discovery or amplify noise. That is a live research question, not a settled result.

---

## V.

This is the one case in the book that has no ending yet, and reading it honestly means saying so. As this is written, in the late summer of 2026, the war has not resolved. A ceasefire around late June took WTI back toward $70, but it has been fragile and repeatedly violated — a cargo ship attacked near Oman in the strait, drone attacks, recurring fears the truce will collapse — and oil has re-elevated to the high seventies and low eighties, well above the $57 the year opened at. The situation oscillates rather than settles: each violation pushes oil up, each de-escalation pulls it back.

Sitting in that, we are squarely in the market's weak channel, and the honesty rules bite hardest. Oil in the low eighties is a probability-weighted guess that will be wrong in one direction — it embeds the crowd's odds that the ceasefire holds against the odds it breaks, and it will look either too high or too low once the next decision resolves. The informative moments are still the resolutions, not today's level: an actual re-closure, or a genuinely durable ceasefire, will reprice oil fast and correctly, as the closure did in March and the pause did in June. The prediction markets say the crowd does not think it is over — US entry by year-end still near ninety per cent, Hormuz traffic normal by September only around forty-six — which is the anticipation read, now observable, and still thin enough to watch rather than trust.

The one thing that can be stated with confidence is the structure, not the outcome. The tail is Hormuz. It is survivable and collectable, unlike a nuclear tail, so the market *can* price it — and it will hold a large premium only while the strait is actually shut. How it ends is unknown, and the discipline is to say so, not to dress an anticipation guess as a forecast.

---

## VI.

Iran is where the whole arc converges. The pre-1914 chapters found the signal in a specific exposed instrument — the *fonds russes*, the Berlin bank balances, the Vienna rate — and here it is the barrel, carrying a war that the equity index waves through. The modern chapters found that markets price the resolution and not the forecast, and here the run-up of June 2025 over-prices a disruption that never comes while every taken decision reprices cleanly. The un-priceable ceiling holds from the other side: Hormuz shows up in the price precisely because it is survivable, where a nuclear exchange would not.

What Iran adds is the first instrument that reads the weak channel directly. For a century the anticipation problem was that a price could not tell you whether men would decide to fight. Prediction markets do not solve that — they are noisy and gameable, and the resolution still carries the cleanest signal — but they route around it enough to change the practice: read the odds for the probability, the barrel for the consequence, and the gap between them for the trade. A calm oil price is still not a forecast of peace, exactly as a calm money market was not in July 1914. But now, for the first time, you can turn to a second screen and see what the crowd thinks the decision will be — and, watching this case unfold in real time, watch the odds move the barrel and the barrel move the odds, in a loop the men of 1914 could not have imagined and would have recognised at once.

## NOTES

*What the prose sets aside — the flags, the live status, and the sources.*

**On the price series.** The WTI and S&P moves are from FRED daily series (`DCOILWTICO`, `SP500`), reproducible from the identifiers in `modern_iran_2026_prediction_markets.md`. The June 2025 figures (Israel strike 13 June, the $75.89 peak on 18 June, the fall to $69 on the US strike, the $65.45 ceasefire close) and the 2026 figures (the ~52% March surge, the ~$114 WTI peak, the hold above $100 through April–May, the S&P ~6575 April dip and ~7600 June high, the $57 January base and the high-$70s/low-$80s re-elevation) are read from those series and flagged for verification against the primary release.

**On the prediction-market figures.** The Polymarket and Kalshi odds and pool sizes (US entry ~90%, invasion 36% on 18 April, Kharg 31%, Hormuz-normal-by-1-September ~46%) are point-in-time, and prediction-market prices move fast — cite each with its date. They are drawn from contemporaneous reporting (Al Jazeera and Bloomberg on the 2026 Hormuz crisis; *Bitcoin.com News*, *Seeking Alpha*, *Frontierbeat* on the Polymarket/Kalshi markets) and should be checked against the platforms' own records. The markets are thin and manipulable; treat a moving odds line as a lead indicator, not proof.

**On the live status.** This is the only case in the book that was unresolved at the time of writing (late summer 2026). Everything in section V is a real-time application, not a post-mortem, and is written to be superseded by events. The claim that will survive is structural — the tail is Hormuz, it is priceable because survivable, and it holds a premium only while the strait is shut — not any statement about how the war ends.

**On the decomposition, stated carefully.** That the oil war-premium can be read as `P(disruption) × size`, with the probability off the prediction market and the size off the futures curve, is an analytical convenience, not an identity the market computes. The reflexive loop between the two screens means they are not independent measurements, which is precisely the new caution this case introduces.
