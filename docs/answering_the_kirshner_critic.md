# Answering the Kirshner critic

*How the book replies to the sharpest objection: "these cases are just finance wanting
peace/stability — you've re-proved* Appeasing Bankers *with better OCR."*

The objection comes in two strengths. The weak form: the war-scare cases (Agadir's Deutsche
Bank pressing the Foreign Office, the Munich relief rally) are dove behavior, so the book merely
re-confirms Kirshner. The strong form turns even the *recovery* cases against us: France floated
the 1871–72 indemnity loans **because ending the occupation restored stability** — a
stability-preference, i.e. a dove motive — so the finance-as-state-instrument reading collapses
back into war-aversion too.

Both are worth taking seriously. Here is the reply.

## 1. Concede what is fair

- Some cases **are** dove cases, and standing alone they would just re-prove Kirshner:
  the **Munich relief rally** (the market marking assets up when war is averted) and
  **Deutsche Bank pressing the Auswärtiges Amt in 1911** (his dove caught in the act).
- The **French withdrawal from Berlin (1911)** is genuinely ambiguous: defensive flight-to-safety
  leaves the identical footprint to a deliberate squeeze, and "scared money running home" is a
  war-aversion reading. We flag it as effect, not proven intent.
- On **1871**, the motive concession is real: a sovereign, un-occupied France is a safer credit
  than an occupied one, so both return-seeking **and** a preference for order are in the
  subscription. We do not pretend the motive was martial.

If the book were *only* these, the critic would be right.

## 2. Motive ≠ the role finance played

Kirshner's dove **restrains** — it stays its own government's hand. The 1871 loan did the
opposite: it **enabled** a state objective (removing a foreign army from French soil). A purse
that funds your goal is not a dove that grips your wrist. "Wanting stability" describes the
investors' *mood*; it does not describe what the instrument *did* — which was to hand the French
state capacity it then spent. **Restraint and capacity are opposite roles**, and the objection
reads the mood onto the role.

The mechanisms differ too. Kirshner's is **lobbying** (bankers push governments toward
appeasement); ours is mostly **pricing** (a bond market discounts expected loss). A relief rally
is *revealed expectation*, not *exercised influence*. Being priced is not the same as being
decisive.

## 3. The falsifiability knife (the spine of the reply)

Watch what "finance wanted stability" must swallow to stay true across the case set:

| Case | What finance did | Critic's gloss |
|------|------------------|----------------|
| 1871–72 | **Lent** to end the occupation | stability |
| Berlin 1911 | **Withdrew** balances | flight to safety → stability |
| Agadir / Munich | **Priced** the scare, then rallied | relief → stability |

Lending, withholding, and fleeing all count as "wanting peace." **A hypothesis that predicts
every observation explains none of them.** A stability-preference that absorbs every direction
of capital flow is not a theory — it is a tautology.

The way out of a tautology is to ask what finance would look like if it *weren't* dovish, and
the case set already answers:

- **The discrimination nulls.** War-aversion predicts finance recoils from *war*. The market
  didn't. It **ignored the Second Balkan War** (an actual war), ignored localized wars, ignored
  pure diplomatic scares (Bosnia 1908–09: null), and stirred **only** for crises that could go
  *general* (Agadir modest, the 1912 mobilization winter real). A peace *preference* has no
  reason to produce **systematic nulls on real wars**; a **probability-weighted risk calculus**
  does. It is the nulls, not the hits, that break the reduction — which is why the case set
  deliberately keeps the duds in.
- **The magnitude.** Even the general-war premia were **modest once detrended** — the dramatic
  raw moves were the business cycle. Finance was war-averse *weakly and selectively*. Kirshner
  asserts the caution steered policy; we measure it and it is small.
- **The state-wielded weapon.** The **1906 Russian loan** (France buying Algeciras alignment)
  and the **coercive squeezes** (Ruhr 1923, the 1941 asset freeze, Suez, 2022) are finance
  deployed *as a weapon, over banker reluctance* — cases where Kirshner himself concedes the
  doves are *overridden*. That is definitionally not "bankers wanting peace."

## 4. The stability France bought was *revanchist* (the 1871 reply, decisive)

This is what breaks the 1871-as-dove reading cleanly. The **same restored credit** that paid the
indemnity became, within fifteen years, the **Franco-Russian loan machine**: French savings
deliberately channeled through the Paris Bourse into Russian state bonds (state-encouraged, from
~1888) to build a military counterweight to Germany — and **1906 is that exact move, already
sourced in this repo**: the great Russian loan floated on the Paris market as Russia backed
France at Algeciras.

So the arc is continuous: **1871–72 rebuilds the purse; 1906/1913 deploys it strategically.**
The "stability" France sought was not systemic pacification but clawing back position in a system
it intended to *revise* (Alsace-Lorraine, the encirclement of Germany). **Finance restoring a
great power's capacity to contest is not the dove — it is the armorer.** Getting the Germans out
was step one of preparing for the next round, not a vote for peace.

That "system-stabilizing finance" is a real but *different* thing is shown by the clean contrast
case: the **1924 Dawes loans**, international capital deployed to stabilize a *rival's* currency
for genuinely systemic reasons. *That* stabilizes the system. 1871 is one power rearming its
balance sheet. The objection conflates "restoring order at home" with "preferring peace abroad";
the Franco-Russian sequel is the receipt that they are not the same.

## 5. The reframe that inoculates the book

Do **not** claim "finance is a weapon, not a dove" — that overclaims and invites the objection.
Claim instead:

> The market reveals a **risk calculus, not a peace preference**. Where bankers were doves
> (Agadir), we catch them in the act and **measure** that the caution was *selective and modest*.
> Where the state wielded finance (1906, the squeezes), the dove was **overridden** — a face
> Kirshner's frame does not contain. And where finance rebuilt a great power (1871), the stability
> it bought was the platform for the next confrontation, not a vote for peace.

**One-liner:** *Kirshner says bankers wanted peace; wanting peace can't explain what the market
ignored, how faintly it flinched, the crises where finance was made to fight — or the fact that
the purse France refilled in 1871 became the alliance it armed against Germany.* Wanting your
house back is not pacifism when you rebuild it as a fortress.
