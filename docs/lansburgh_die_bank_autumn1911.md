# Lansburgh in *Die Bank* on the autumn-1911 (Agadir) money-market crisis

A German-press corroboration of Chapter III, from the man who edited *Die Bank* and
ran its money-market commentary. Alfred Lansburgh's autumn-1911 post-mortem is a
contemporary, quantitative account of the very Berlin credit squeeze the chapter
narrates — written from inside Berlin, not from the London/New York press we had been
using.

## Provenance & caveats

- **Source deposit:** "Articles by Alfred Lansburgh in *Die Bank* (1908–1935)," Zenodo
  record **5115892** (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.5115892), one 11.2 MB zip, **CC-BY-4.0**,
  deposited 2021-07-20. 714 individual article `.txt` files under `Artikel/`, plus
  per-year concatenations under `Jahre/`.
- **Public-domain basis:** Lansburgh died in **1937**, so his work entered the German
  public domain in **2008** (70 yrs p.m.a.) — a cleaner basis than "published 1911."
- **It is Lansburgh only.** Other *Die Bank* contributors and the journal's statistical
  tables are **not** in the deposit. So this gives his commentary, not the run of rate
  tables.
- **Treat figures as OCR-grade.** These are plain-text transcriptions, not page images.
  Every number below is located in the text but should be **verified against the
  original page image** before it goes in the book (same discipline we used for the
  *Chronicle*). Page ranges are in each filename (e.g. `… 2 1160-1168`).

## Frequency: *Die Bank* was **monthly**, not weekly

The pasted source called it *Wochenhefte* (weekly); that is wrong. *Die Bank* was
**Monatshefte für Finanz- und Bankwesen** — a monthly. The deposit carries no issue
dates, so this rests on the standard bibliographic title plus the cadence visible in
the files: ~29 Lansburgh pieces across 1911, in **two half-year Bände** (Band 1 ≈ pp
1–550, Band 2 ≈ pp 600–1175) of continuous pagination, and the autumn-crisis article is
a **single retrospective** covering September–November 1911 (it discusses the end-Oct
bank balance sheets "published on the last day of November"), i.e. a December-1911
monthly number. A weekly would not review the whole autumn in one late piece. This
matters for the chapter: Lansburgh tracks the crisis month-by-month, not week-by-week.

## The central article

**"Die Zahlungsbereitschaft der Berliner Banken im Herbst 1911"** (Band 2, pp
1160–1168) — "The liquidity of the Berlin banks in autumn 1911." Lansburgh's own
quantitative post-mortem. Key claims (German verbatim → gloss; figures flagged for
verification):

1. **How close to a credit crisis.** *"…wie nahe wir einer Kreditkrisis gewesen sind."*
   — how near Germany came to a **credit crisis** (he treats the war-scare and the
   credit-scare as the two faces of the danger just past).

2. **A deposit run in September.** *"Bekanntlich hat sich im September ein intensiver
   Rückfluss der privaten Gelder von den Kreditinstituten in die Geldschränke und
   Taschen des Publikums vollzogen… eine ausgesprochene Bankenflucht der Gelder."* — an
   intense flight of private money out of the banks into the public's cash-boxes over
   several weeks, starting at the savings banks and ending at the big banks.

3. **The French withdrawal — the mechanism behind Berlin's stringency.** *"Dazu kam
   dass die französischen Banken und diejenigen sonstigen Gläubiger Deutschlands die
   Zahlungen nach Frankreich zu leisten hatten ihre ausländischen Guthaben abhoben und
   die deutschen Banken so erheblicher weiterer Mittel beraubten."* — French banks and
   other creditors withdrew their balances held in Germany, stripping the German banks
   of further means. This is the German-side confirmation of the "French pulling
   balances from Berlin" that the *Chronicle* reported from outside.

4. **Magnitude [VERIFY].** Foreign monies (*fremde Gelder*) at all eight big banks fell
   **≈ 480.3 million M** vs 31 Aug — *"annähernd ½ Milliarde M… 9½% des
   Gesamtbestandes."* **Deutsche Bank alone lost 224 M (12½%)** — which, Lansburgh
   notes, explains why it pressed the **Auswärtiges Amt** (Foreign Office) for a calming
   statement at end-September.

5. **The Reichsbank pushed to its rationing limit by mid-September.** *"…schon Mitte
   September sind die Banken nicht mehr weit von dem Punkte entfernt gewesen wo die
   Reichsbank ihre Diskont- und Lombard-Einreichungen mit besonderer Aufmerksamkeit auf
   Ursprung und Zweckbestimmung zu kontrollieren beginnt."* — by **mid-September** the
   banks were near the point where the Reichsbank begins scrutinising (rationing) their
   discount/Lombard paper. From mid-Sept to end-Oct they rediscounted and lombarded
   *"erheblich mehr als im Jahre zuvor und sogar mehr als im Herbst des bösen Jahres
   1907"* — more than the year before and even more than in the autumn of the "bad year"
   **1907**.

6. **Flight to liquidity — even state bonds unsaleable.** *"In den kritischen Tagen des
   September und Oktober wären nicht einmal deutsche Staatsanleihen ohne grossen Verlust
   zu realisieren gewesen."* — in the critical days of Sept–Oct **not even German state
   loans could have been sold without heavy loss**. This is the exact
   flight-to-liquidity confound raised earlier in the bond work, now documented
   contemporaneously for Berlin.

7. **The stock-market slump was partly bank policy.** The banks met nearly two-thirds
   of their need by cutting stock-exchange *Report* (call) loans — *"weit über 300
   Millionen Mark"* withdrawn from the Bourse in a few weeks [VERIFY] — forcing the
   September price collapse; itself driven, he stresses, by depositors' war fears
   (*Kriegsbefürchtungen*).

8. **The quarter-end is the frame.** Lansburgh repeatedly anchors the crisis on the
   autumn **quarter-end** settlement: *"die grossen Quartalsansprüche im Herbst"* and
   *"so wichtige Termine wie der 31. März und der 30. September wo die grössten
   Anforderungen an die Banken… gestellt werden."*

9. **German dependence on foreign short credit.** The banks could not place their
   acceptances *"angesichts der ablehnenden Haltung des französischen Geldmarktes des
   besten Abnehmers für deutsche Bankakzepte"* — because the **French money market, the
   best buyer of German bank acceptances, had turned away.**

## Other directly relevant Lansburgh articles (1911–12)

- **"Kriegskostendeckung"** (Bd 2, 707–717) — war-finance. Opens: *"Wie wird Deutschland
  seinen nächsten Krieg führen — mit Steuern, ungedeckten Banknoten oder Anleihen? Schon
  lange bevor man ernstlich… rechnen begann dass Deutschland um Marokkos Willen zu den
  Waffen greifen könnte…"* — ties the war-finance question directly to **Morocco/Agadir**.
- **"Die Creditrestriction der Reichsbank"** (Bd 1, 527–534, ~mid-1911, *before* the
  crisis) — the Reichsbank Directorate already objecting to the big banks' *"stossweises
  Auftreten an den Terminen"* (lurching demands at the settlement dates). Shows the
  quarter-end strain on the Reichsbank was structural, pre-dating Agadir.
- **"Centrifugale Bewegung am Kapitalmarkt"** (Bd 2, 919–933) — capital flight in the
  crisis (mentions Marokko).
- **"Der Berliner Privatdiskont"** (1912, Bd 1, 322–328) and **"Privatdiskont und
  Bankdiskont"** (1912, Bd 2, 733–740) — Lansburgh on the Berlin **private-discount**
  market itself; relevant to the spot-vs-to-arrive work.
- **"Spargelder und Kriegsflucht"** (1912, Bd 2, 1147–1153) — savings and "war-flight"
  of capital, the Balkan-crisis analogue.

## What is **not** in Lansburgh (so we don't over-claim)

- **No explicit "19 September" Reichsbank rate figure.** His autumn article gives the
  *mechanism* (mid-September rationing, the rediscount/Lombard surge), not the headline
  discount-rate move or its exact date. The rate number must come from a rate series
  (Neal–Weidenmier Berlin bank rate) or the *Chronicle*, not from this deposit.
- **No rate tables.** The deposit is his prose only.
- **No named individual.** Lansburgh writes only *"dieses erste deutsche Institut"* (the
  Deutsche Bank) — checked the full article: no *Herr*, no *Direktor*, no personal name.

## Who from Deutsche Bank pressed the Foreign Office? — an honest attribution

The sourced record names the **institution, not a person**: Lansburgh says *the Deutsche
Bank* pressed the *Auswärtiges Amt* for a calming declaration at end-September 1911; *Le
Temps* (30 Sep 1911) says *"les directeurs des grandes banques de Berlin"* received the
government's assurances. No accessible primary source — Lansburgh, *Le Temps*, or the
secondary historiography searched — names the individual for this specific approach.

- **The overwhelmingly likely man is Arthur von Gwinner** — **Spokesman of Deutsche Bank's
  Management Board 1910–1919** (so its head in 1911), responsible for international
  operations and known within the bank as **"the bank's diplomat"** (Deutsche Bank
  Historical Association). If one director carried the bank's alarm to the Wilhelmstrasse,
  it was almost certainly Gwinner. But this is **role-based inference, not a documented
  act** — no source places Gwinner personally at the Foreign Office in that week.
- **The counterparty** was the Foreign Office of State Secretary **Alfred von
  Kiderlen-Wächter**, architect of the *Panthersprung*.

**Manuscript rule:** write *"the Deutsche Bank — whose board spokesman and 'diplomat' was
Arthur von Gwinner — pressed Kiderlen's Foreign Office for reassurance,"* which is exactly
what the evidence supports. Do **not** write *"Gwinner went to Kiderlen"* as fact; the line
between the documented institution and the inferred individual is real. Pinning the person
would need a specialised source (a Deutsche Bank corporate history, or the *Große Politik*
/ Auswärtiges-Amt files for late September 1911).

## Bearing on Chapter III — and on our spot-vs-to-arrive reversal

Lansburgh **strengthens** the chapter's substance while **confirming** our narrower
statistical caution:

- He independently documents, from Berlin, the **French balance-withdrawal**, the
  **deposit run**, the **Reichsbank rationing**, and the **flight to liquidity (even
  state bonds)** — the crisis was real and severe ("near a credit crisis").
- But he frames it as an **amplified autumn quarter-end** — the *"Quartalsansprüche im
  Herbst,"* the 30 September *Termin*. That is exactly why our weekly spot-vs-to-arrive
  finding came out the way it did: the forward-bill gap is a **quarter-end** premium
  (present in war-free 1910 too), and in 1911 the ordinary quarter-end squeeze was
  amplified by the French withdrawal and the deposit run. The gap alone cannot separate
  the war-amplification from the seasonal base — Lansburgh says as much in prose, locating
  the crisis *on top of* the normal autumn settlement. So the honest chapter claim is
  **credit withdrawal + contemporary belief, documented in Lansburgh's own words**, with
  the money-market rates used to show Berlin was severely strained at the 1911 turn —
  not that a unique rate signature isolates war risk.
