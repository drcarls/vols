# Modern run — Iran 2026: the tail materialized, and prediction markets priced the decision

Two developments the framework did not have before, both live in 2026:

1. **The Hormuz tail *materialized*** — where June 2025 only threatened it. This is the natural
   experiment the framework predicted: a *threat* vs an *actual closure* should look completely
   different in the oil price, and they do.
2. **Prediction markets now quantify the *anticipation* channel** — the probability of the
   political-military decision that asset prices historically could not cleanly reveal. This is a
   genuine methodological change to how war risk is priced, and it maps precisely onto the book's
   anticipation-vs-resolution distinction.

## What happened (2026)

A US–Israel–Iran war erupted in **late February 2026**; the **Strait of Hormuz was virtually shut**
(~8 million bbl/day removed). Oil surged **~52% in March** — WTI to a **$114 peak** (Brent ~$113) —
and, crucially, **held above $100 through April–May**, not the days-long spike-and-collapse of June
2025.

**Status as of August 2026 — live and unresolved.** This is the *one case in the whole arc that is
not closed.* A ceasefire around late June brought WTI back to ~$70, but it is **fragile and
repeatedly violated** — a cargo ship attacked near Oman in the Strait, drone attacks, ceasefire-end
fears — and oil has **re-elevated to ~$75–82** (Brent ~$79) through early August, still well above
the **$57** January base. The situation is **oscillating**, not settled: each violation pushes oil
up, each de-escalation pulls it back. Everything below is therefore a *real-time* application, not a
post-mortem — and that is the hardest test the framework faces.

- **The size and *duration* discriminated threat from reality.** June 2025 (Hormuz threatened, strait
  open): oil spiked +10% and unwound *below* pre-crisis within days. 2026 (Hormuz actually closed):
  oil +100% and sustained for two months. **The same instrument, two magnitudes — because the tail
  was real this time.** That is the discrimination test operating on the *oil* rung, in real time.
- **Equities discriminated too.** The S&P *dipped* to ~6575 in April but **recovered to new highs
  (~7600) by June**, even with a Middle East war and Hormuz shut — priced as an **energy/localized**
  shock, not a systemic equity event. The 2022 finding, replayed.

## Prediction markets — the anticipation channel made observable

This is the new thing, and it is important for the book. Historically the market's **weak channel**
was anticipation — you could never cleanly read *"will they go to war?"* off an asset price, because
oil blends the *probability* of disruption with its *size*, and equities blend war with the cycle.
In 2026, prediction markets price the **decision itself**, in the open, with real money:

- **Polymarket:** *US forces enter Iran by 31 Dec* — **~90%** ($115M); **Iran invasion — 36%** the
  day Iran fully closed Hormuz (18 Apr); **Kharg Island** (Iran's main oil terminal) **31%** to fall
  by 30 June ($12M).
- **Kalshi:** *normal Hormuz traffic by 1 Sep* — **~46%** ($19.7M); a separate "closed 7+ days"
  market ~$7.3M.

And they **feed back into oil pricing**: contemporaneous reporting is explicit that *"prediction
markets price in Hormuz reopening after the US strike pause, signaling lower oil prices"* — the
**odds moved first, and oil followed.** For the first time you can **decompose the oil price**:

> oil war-premium ≈ **P(disruption)** — read off Polymarket/Kalshi — **× size of disruption** — read
> off the oil futures curve.

The pre-1914 economist (and the 1991 or even 2022 one) had only the product; the 2026 one can see
the two factors separately, and watch them move.

## The framework's own tests, applied to prediction markets

Turning the book's lenses on this new instrument:

- **Discrimination, but finer-grained.** Prediction markets discriminate *among sub-scenarios* the
  blunt oil price cannot — US entry vs invasion vs Hormuz-days vs Kharg falling. They price the
  *structure* of the risk, not just its magnitude.
- **Do they also cry wolf?** The open question. Early 2026 evidence is that they were reasonably
  **calibrated and fast** (the invasion odds rose as Hormuz closed; the reopening odds rose on the
  strike pause, leading oil). But they are **thin and manipulable** (Polymarket's known manipulation
  episodes), so the "cried wolf" discipline now applies to the *prediction* market too: a moving odds
  line is not proof, and a large bet can be a signal or a spoof.
- **Resolution still dominates.** Even with prediction markets, the cleanest, most accurate repricing
  is at the **decision point** (Hormuz actually closing; the strike pause). Prediction markets improve
  the *anticipation* read; they do not overturn the finding that markets price a *taken* decision best.
- **Reflexivity — the new caution.** Because oil traders watch the odds and bettors watch oil, there
  is a **feedback loop** that did not exist in 1914. It can sharpen price discovery (decision-relevant
  information aggregated faster) or amplify noise (a manipulated odds line nudging the oil price).
  A live research question, not a settled result.

## Lessons for today (updating the synthesis)

- **The anticipation problem is now partly observable.** The century-old weakness — markets can't
  price an undecided decision — is being **routed around** by prediction markets that price the
  decision directly. Read them for the *probability*, oil for the *consequence*.
- **But the hierarchy holds.** Prediction markets are better *forecasters* than oil, yet still noisy
  and manipulable; the **resolution** repricing remains the highest-quality signal. Use prediction
  markets to watch the anticipation channel, not to trust it.
- **For an Iran war specifically:** the tradeable tail is **Hormuz**, and 2026 shows exactly how it
  reads — oil holds a large premium *only while the strait is actually shut*, and both the odds and
  the price unwind on de-escalation, with the **odds leading**. A calm oil price still is not a
  forecast of peace; but now you can check the Polymarket/Kalshi line to see what the crowd thinks
  the *decision* will be.

## Reading it in real time (August 2026) — the discipline the live case demands

Because it is unresolved, we are sitting squarely in the market's **weak channel — anticipation** —
and the framework's honesty rules bite hardest here:

- **Oil at ~$82 is a probability-weighted guess that will be wrong in one direction.** It embeds the
  crowd's odds that the fragile ceasefire holds vs breaks. The framework says do *not* read it as a
  forecast — it is the anticipation channel doing the thing it does poorly (oscillating with each
  violation), and it will look either too high or too low once the next decision resolves.
- **The informative moments are still the resolutions**, not today's level: an *actual* Hormuz
  closure, or a *genuine* durable ceasefire, will reprice oil fast and correctly — as the strait's
  closure did in March and the pause did in June. Watch the events, not the ambient price.
- **The prediction markets say the crowd thinks it isn't over** (US entry by 31 Dec still ~90%;
  Hormuz normal by 1 Sep only ~46%) — the anticipation read, now observable, but *thin and
  manipulable*, so a lead indicator to watch, not to trust.
- **The one thing we can state with confidence is the structure, not the outcome:** the tail is
  Hormuz; it is *survivable and collectable*, so the market *can* price it; and it will hold a large
  premium **only while the strait is actually shut.** How it ends is unknown — and the discipline is
  to say so, not to dress an anticipation guess as a forecast.
