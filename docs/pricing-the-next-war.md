# PRICING THE NEXT WAR
### What markets know about geopolitical risk — and what they structurally cannot

*The applied companion to* Not This Year*, a book about money and the nine years Europe spent not
going to war before 1914. This chapter stands alone. It is written for someone who prices risk for a
living, and it makes one argument: markets are poor at forecasting whether a war will happen and very
good at pricing its consequences once the decision is taken — and the gap between those two skills is
where most of the mistakes, and some of the opportunities, live.*

---

Here is the single most useful thing the historical record has to say to a trading desk, and it is
counterintuitive enough that people rediscover it, expensively, every few years.

On the morning of 17 January 1991, the United States began bombing Iraq. Oil, which had nearly
doubled since Saddam invaded Kuwait the previous August, fell thirty per cent — its largest one-day
decline on record. The S&P rose about four per cent. The Bank of England, in its quarterly bulletin,
wrote the explanation down in plain language: equities and bonds rose sharply "as news of early
successes for the allies encouraged participants back into the market… the largest-ever one day
decline in oil prices helped the rallies."

The war did not end that day. It had just started. What ended was the *uncertainty* — the fat tail
the market had been carrying since August, in which Saddam torched the Gulf's oil fields and closed
the Strait of Hormuz. Allied air supremacy from the first night took that tail off the table, and the
premium built to price it evaporated in a session. Buy the invasion, sell the phony war.

That is not a quirk of 1991. It is the mechanism, and once you see it you see it everywhere.

## Two different skills, routinely confused

A market does two very different things when a war looms, and it is good at exactly one of them.

The first is **anticipation**: pricing the probability that a war happens at all. This is forecasting
a political decision that, in most cases, the decision-makers themselves have not yet made — and
markets are bad at it. They are bad at it in a specific, structural way. They cry wolf, because most
scares fizzle and it is rational to discount them. They price the wrong sign: in September 1939 the
US market *rose* twenty-one per cent on the outbreak of the Second World War, because for a neutral
arms supplier the war looked like a boom — and it did not crash until the fall of France, eight months
later, turned a distant profit into a systemic threat. They get blindsided by surprises: Pearl Harbor
drew a first-day drop of under four per cent, partly because a genuine surprise offers nothing to
anticipate, and partly because war risk was already half in the price. And in the summer of 1914, the
canonical case, the money market said nothing at all until the Austrian ultimatum on 23 July — because
the decision had been taken privately on the 5th and kept invisible until then. The market's first
chance to price the crisis was the ultimatum, and the ultimatum was not a step toward the war. It was
the war becoming visible.

The second skill is **resolution**: pricing the *consequences* of a decision once it has been taken.
Here the market is fast, large, and correct. The oil premium collapsing the night Desert Storm began.
The relief rally on the Munich accord in 1938. The market that recovered its whole loss the week of
Inchon in 1950. This is the market's real talent — not prophecy, but arithmetic on a decision already
made.

The practical upshot is a discipline, not a strategy: **do not trade the forecast; trade the
resolution.** A calm market is not a forecast of peace. It is the market declining to handicap a
decision no one has made — which is the precise error a generation of observers made in July 1914,
and the precise error being made about Taiwan today.

## The ladder: what kind of war is it?

Before you ask *how much*, ask *what kind* — because the market sorts wars not by size or bloodshed but
by one criterion: could this go *general*? Could it become the systemic war that drags in the great
powers and takes the whole system down, at which point choosing a side stops mattering?

The pre-1914 money market answered that question with startling consistency, and the twentieth century
answered it again in daily prices. It produces a ladder with four rungs.

**Localised wars, the market ignores.** The Second Balkan War of 1913 — a real war — moved the great
markets not at all. The Falklands barely dented sterling; the damage sat on Argentina, already
unravelling in its own debt crisis. North Korean missile tests spike and fade. India and Pakistan can
fight, and the rupee and regional equity take it while global markets shrug. If a war is localised in
its *impact*, the great markets discount it, and — usefully — here their calm is roughly informative,
because it genuinely stays contained.

**General but judged unlikely, the market discounts.** The Berlin Airlift of 1948–49 was a prolonged
US–USSR standoff, and the American market *rose twenty-one per cent through the war scare* and shrugged
at the blockade. It gave no relief rally when the blockade lifted — because the war had never been in
the price to begin with. (That missing relief rally is a clean diagnostic: resolution rallies only
where the war was actually priced.)

**General and survivable, the market prices — dynamically.** Korea in 1950 is the cleanest case in the
record. It broke thirteen per cent on the June invasion, feared that summer as the opening of a third
world war; recovered the entire loss once Inchon made the war look contained; broke six per cent again
when China's intervention revived the general-war fear; and made new highs once it settled into a
limited war. The *same war* repriced up and down as its probability of going general rose and fell.
This is where oil wars live too — the Gulf, Iraq 2003, the Iran episodes — and it is the rung where
the resolution trade works.

**Existential, the market cannot price at all.** In October 1962, through the most dangerous fortnight
in human history, the S&P fell a few per cent — and its low came *before* the most dangerous days. It
rose on the morning the blockade of Cuba was enforced. The reason is not that the market judged the
risk small. It is that there is no version of the world in which the bearish position pays off: a claim
on a future that, in the catastrophic branch, does not exist. **You cannot price a catastrophe you will
not survive to collect on.** The discrimination test has a ceiling, and existential risk sits above it,
unpriced not because it is improbable but because the instrument cannot represent it.

Keep that ceiling in mind. It is the most important thing on the board for anyone thinking about
Taiwan.

## Read the exposed instrument, not the index

The second discipline is that the signal does not live in the index. It lives in whichever instrument
is directly exposed, and that instrument is different in every war.

In the Gulf and over Iran it is **oil**. In 2022 it was **energy** — the S&P actually closed *up* on
the day Russia invaded Ukraine, and the VIX peaked at a mere 36, because for Western equity the war was
an energy shock, not a systemic threat; the war went into Brent (up thirty-four per cent) and into the
ruble, not into the S&P. In 1905 it was a **sovereign bond** — French savers watched their Russian
holdings halve while the French government's own paper sat still, a portfolio crisis invisible to any
index. At Suez in 1956 it was **reserves** — under a fixed exchange rate the pressure showed not in a
price but in Britain's draining gold and the denial of IMF access, which is what actually forced the
withdrawal.

A generalist watching the S&P through any of these would have seen almost nothing until it was over.
The person watching the exposed instrument saw the whole thing. This has a direct corollary for the
next war: **identify the instrument before the crisis, not during it.** Which brings us to the two
that matter most right now.

## Iran: the tail you can actually trade

Iran is the clean, live example of the whole framework, because the exposed instrument — oil — is
liquid, and the tail — the Strait of Hormuz — is survivable and collectable, so the market *can* price
it.

Watch how it behaved across two episodes. In June 2025, Israel and then the United States struck Iran;
oil spiked about ten per cent on the fear that Hormuz would close. Then Iran's response was telegraphed
and symbolic, the strait stayed open, and oil fell *below* where it had started, while equities made
new highs. The tail was priced, then removed, then unwound — in days. Then in the spring of 2026 the
tail actually *materialised*: Hormuz was shut, roughly eight million barrels a day came off the market,
and oil ran from the high fifties to a hundred and fourteen dollars and *held above a hundred for two
months*. Same instrument, two completely different behaviours — because the first was a scare and the
second was a real closure, and the size and, crucially, the *duration* of the move discriminated
between them.

That is the tradeable structure. The Hormuz premium is real only while the strait is actually shut; it
collapses the moment the closure is removed; and the market is poor at forecasting whether the closure
comes but fast at pricing it once it does. A calm oil price is not a forecast that nothing happens —
it is the market declining to handicap Tehran's undecided choice.

And there is now a new instrument pointed at exactly the channel markets are worst at. Prediction
markets — Polymarket, Kalshi — price the *decision itself*: as of this writing the crowd puts US forces
entering Iran near ninety per cent and normal Hormuz traffic by September under half, with tens of
millions of dollars staked. For the first time you can *decompose the war premium* — read the
probability of disruption off the prediction market, the size off the oil curve — and in the spring of
2026 the reopening odds moved first and the oil price followed. Treat that instrument with the same
suspicion you would any thin, manipulable market: it can cry wolf, and the resolution still dominates.
But it is genuine new information in the one place the market has always been blind.

## Taiwan: the tail you cannot

Taiwan is the important case precisely because the intuitions everyone carries about it are wrong, and
the framework says why.

A war over Taiwan is a candidate *general* war — US against China, possibly nuclear — which puts it
near the Cuba end of the ladder, not the Gulf. So two things follow. First, the market will be poor at
pricing it in advance, because it is a decision Beijing has not made; and second, to the degree it
approaches the existential, it drifts toward *un-priceable*. Both mean the same operational warning:
**calm markets are not evidence a Taiwan war is unlikely.** They are the July-1914 signature.

The data we have fits this exactly. When Nancy Pelosi visited Taiwan in August 2022 and China answered
with its largest-ever blockade drills — missiles over the island, a rehearsal of quarantine — the
markets barely moved. Semiconductors, the exposed instrument, took the biggest hit at minus three and
a half per cent; the S&P fell under one per cent; everything was *higher* within a fortnight. The
market discounted that episode as a drill, and was right that time. But a three-and-a-half-per-cent
semis wobble that reverses in two weeks tells you the market judged *that episode* would stay below the
general threshold. It tells you nothing about a priced invasion. If Beijing decided, the repricing
would come at the resolution, be violent, and land in chips and Chinese assets first — not in the
ambient calm beforehand.

Note the instrument. For Taiwan it is **semiconductors** — the island fabricates the overwhelming
majority of the world's advanced chips, so a Taiwan war is a *silicon* shock the way a Gulf war is an
*oil* shock. Watch the semiconductor complex and the Taiwan dollar and the yuan; the S&P is the last
place the signal will show. And treat the direct-war tail the way you treat any existential risk: it
is not a spot-premium you can harvest, it is a convexity question, because the market structurally
cannot put it in the price. The absence of a Taiwan premium is not the absence of the danger. It is the
instrument hitting its ceiling.

## Finance as a weapon — and why the weapon is decaying

There is one more move on the modern board that the pre-1914 world only hinted at: finance deployed not
as a barometer but as an outright weapon. The lineage is three freezes. In 1941 the United States froze
Japanese assets and embargoed oil, and cornered Japan toward war. In 1956 it denied Britain the
sterling support and IMF access it needed, and forced a great power to abort a war it was winning. In
2022 the G7 froze roughly three hundred billion dollars of Russian central-bank reserves — and it
punished without halting. One weapon, three outcomes: provoked, coerced, punished. The effect is
documented in every case; the *decisiveness* varies and must never be assumed.

For a markets person the important dynamic is what the 2022 freeze set off, because its most important
audience was Beijing. China holds a vast dollar reserve and, until recently, an enormous Treasury
position — and it watched a peer's reserves immobilised overnight. It has been hardening ever since:
the People's Bank has been buying gold for twenty consecutive months, to some 2,346 tonnes, and kept
buying straight through a sixteen-per-cent price slide, which is the signature of structural
rebalancing rather than a trade. Gold is still under ten per cent of its reserves against roughly
seventy for Western central banks, so there is a great deal of room left. Meanwhile its Treasury
holdings have fallen to around six hundred and ninety billion dollars, the lowest since 2008 and half
the 2013 peak, and it is building payment rails — CIPS, the digital yuan — that route around SWIFT.

The logic is precise: **a Treasury bond is a hostage; a bar of gold in your own vault is not.** Gold is
the anti-freeze asset — a claim on no one, held at home, settled without permission. So the gold pile
and the Treasury run-off are one move — sell the hostage, buy what cannot be seized. And the
implication is the one the historian recognises instantly, because it is the whole engine of the book
this chapter accompanies: **the coercive value of the US financial weapon over China is a wasting
asset.** Every tonne of gold and every payment node reduces the future force of a freeze. Germany,
squeezed at Agadir in 1911, spent the next three years drawing gold into the vault and reforming bank
liquidity — a reform completed the very month the war began, by which point the squeeze could not have
been repeated. China is doing the same reform *ahead* of its crisis rather than after it.

Two disciplines keep this from becoming a story you lose money on. First, do not overclaim that the
hostage is freed: six hundred and ninety billion is still an enormous exposure, gold cannot pay for
imports at the scale of a wartime economy, and the payment rails still lean partly on Western
messaging. China has *reduced* the weapon's bite, not neutralised it. Second — and this is the one the
book insists on hardest — **the gold documents capability, not a decision.** Prudential
reserve-building and preparation for aggression are identical on a balance sheet. China's gold is
consistent with "prudent diversification after watching Russia" and with "making ourselves
sanction-proof for a move on Taiwan," and no balance sheet can tell you which. Anyone reading the gold
as proof of war plans is making the deepest error in the field — reading exposure as intent, capability
as decision.

## What to actually do with this

Strip it to the rules a desk can use.

**One. Don't trade the forecast; trade the resolution.** The market is a poor prophet and a fine
accountant. The signal is at the decision — the blockade declared, the invasion launched, the sanctions
package — not in the ambient level before it. Positioning for *whether* is a coin flip dressed as
analysis; positioning for the repricing at the decision is the edge.

**Two. Buy the invasion, sell the phony war.** When a survivable war actually starts and the feared
catastrophe does not materialise, the premium built to price it collapses — the Gulf in 1991, Iraq in
2003, Iran in 2025. The most reliable geopolitical trade is the mean-reversion of a tail that gets
resolved, not the spike itself.

**Three. Trade the exposed instrument.** Oil for the Gulf and Iran, energy for Russia, semiconductors
for Taiwan, reserves and currencies for the coercion cases. The index is where the signal goes to hide.

**Four. Respect the ceiling.** For the wars that could end the game — a full US–China war, the nuclear
tails — the risk is not in the price and cannot be, so do not read its absence as safety and do not try
to harvest a premium that isn't there. That is a convexity and tail-hedge problem, not a spot trade.

**Five. Watch the weapon decay.** The financial weapon is a wasting asset as targets harden. Position
for a world in which the freeze works a little less each year, and in which the gold price carries a
structural central-bank bid that has nothing to do with real yields.

And the one that ties them together, the sentence the whole record keeps repeating: **a calm market is
not a forecast of peace.** It is the market declining to price a decision no one has made — and the
job, the one the market cannot do for you, is to notice when a decision is about to be made anyway.
