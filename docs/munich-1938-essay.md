# THE RELIEF
### Paris, September 1938

*Modern companion to "The Squeeze," "Spent," and "The Shrug." The working evidence — the dated Le Temps Bulletin financier arc from Gallica and the single exchange anchor — is in `prototype_munich_1938.md`. Notes at the end carry what the prose sets aside, and this chapter sets aside more than most.*

---

On the thirtieth of September 1938 the Paris market rallied on the news that there would be no war. It was right about September and wrong about history, and the gap between those two judgements is the whole of what a market can and cannot do.

It was right about September because the Munich Agreement had been signed in the small hours; Czechoslovakia had been made to give up the Sudetenland without a seat at the table; Chamberlain was flying home to wave a piece of paper and promise peace for our time. The war that had looked certain for a fortnight was off, the franc firmed, the rente steadied, and the finance column of *Le Temps* recorded a market that had *manifesté avec éclat un optimisme* it had never *complètement abandonné*.

It was wrong about history because within six months German troops were in Prague, within a year the war the relief had celebrated avoiding was general, and the piece of paper was a byword. But the market had not been asked about history. It had been asked whether this crisis, this week, would end in war; it answered correctly and went back to business.

---

## I.

For anyone testing whether the pre-1914 method still works, the virtue of Munich is that the crisis wrote itself down day by day in a market report that named the war news each time. Twelve issues of the *Bulletin financier* bracket the arc; four carry a datable verdict on the market's tone, and they fall into the textbook shape of a war scare that ends in relief — build, hope, rally, settle. It is the same anatomy as Agadir a generation earlier, drawn in cleaner, better-dated interwar ink.

The build came on the seventeenth of September. Chamberlain had returned abruptly from Berchtesgaden and Henlein had proclaimed for the Reich; the talks, the column reported, had *fait apparaître des difficultés sérieuses*, and the market's mood was one of *déception évidente* — disappointment, plainly stated on the finance page and tied to the diplomatic breakdown by name.

The turn came on the twenty-eighth, after Hitler's Sportpalast speech the night before, which Paris had stayed up parsing. The market opened *plus calme, quoique très irrégulière* — calmer, though very uneven — because the speech, on reading, *ne contenait rien d'irréparable* and left standing the hope of *une solution pacifique du problème des Sudètes*. The floor was a single sentence in a speech, read for whether it shut the door or left it ajar; it left it ajar, and the market found its footing on the reading.

The rally came on the thirtieth, and the settle on the eighth of October: a *séance calme*, most stocks easing back a little, the market *se conformant aux recommandations de M. Daladier* — falling in behind the government's coming financial measures, and returning to the ordinary business of a country that had decided it was not, after all, going to war that month. The market worked the diplomatic news the way a reader works a barometer.

---

## II.

There is one number in the whole arc, and it is worth pausing on because it is one and not a series. On the day of the accord the franc firmed against both currencies that mattered: the pound eased from a hundred and seventy-eight francs ninety to a hundred and seventy-eight fifty-one, the dollar from thirty-eight ninety to thirty-seven fifty-five. Small against sterling, larger against the dollar, and in the same direction — money that had been leaving the franc for the safety of London and New York turned, for a day, and came partly home, because the war it had been fleeing was called off. That is the relief rally made quantitative: one day, two quotations, the fear priced and unpriced in a single session.

Everything else in the arc is the qualitative report — the column's own word for the tone, dated and attributed, but not a price line — and the chapter should say so plainly. What it has is a market that named the war-or-peace news four times in three weeks and moved with it, plus one clean exchange print on the day of the resolution. What it lacks is the continuous rente-and-equity series that would turn the report into a measured signal. The proof that the method travels into the interwar is here; the full measurement is not, and that difference is kept in the notes rather than smoothed over.

---

## III.

Munich earns its place because it is appeasement's canonical crisis, and appeasement is where the argument about finance and war is usually lost. The standard account runs that the democracies were financially cautious, that caution bred a preference for accommodation, and that the preference produced Munich. It is told in stated preferences — memoranda, cabinet minutes, the private anxieties of finance ministers — and it is persuasive on that evidence. What it has never carried is the price counterpart: the financial community's war-aversion shown not as something men said but as something the market did. The seventeenth of September and the thirtieth are that counterpart, the slide on the breakdown and the rally on the accord, dated and, on the last day, quantified. The market did not merely prefer peace in the abstract; it paid up for it the morning it arrived.

The book's discipline governs this chapter too, and it is hardest to hold exactly where the story is most seductive. That the market wanted peace and priced its arrival is documented. That this war-aversion *caused* Munich — that the rente drove Daladier to the table, or the franc drove Chamberlain onto the plane — is not shown by a relief rally and is not claimed here. A price movement is the market's condition made visible: evidence of what the financial community feared and hoped, a symptom of the moment, not proof that the moment turned on it. The chapter supplies the priced war-aversion the appeasement literature asserts and never measures. It does not supply the causal claim that finance made the men decide, because the evidence does not carry it.

---

## IV.

Munich shows, more cleanly than any crisis in the book, the exact boundary of what a market knows. It was a good accountant of the resolution in front of it: asked whether the Sudeten crisis would end in war, it read the barometer correctly through every turn — down on Berchtesgaden, steady on the speech, up on the accord — and was not wrong on a single reading. Each was a true statement about the week it was made.

It was no kind of prophet of the war beyond the resolution. It did not price, on the thirtieth of September, that the peace it was rallying on was a deferral rather than a settlement, that the same Reich would take the rest of Czechoslovakia in the spring and Poland in the autumn, that the piece of paper was worth what its signer intended it to be worth, which was nothing. It could not have. A market prices the consequences of the decision that has been taken, not the decisions the men have not yet imagined taking; it trades the resolution in front of it and leaves the future to historians, who have the advantage of already knowing how it came out.

This is the distinction the modern trading chapters turn on, in its purest and most tragic form. Anticipation is the weak channel: the market cannot tell you a war is coming next year. Resolution is the strong one: it can tell you, to the day and the exchange rate, that this crisis has just ended in peace. Munich is where the two came apart most cruelly — the market completely right about the resolution, the resolution completely wrong about the future — and both are true at once, and neither is a failure of the market to do the one thing a market does.

---

## V.

The relief rally of the thirtieth of September belongs beside the Agadir relief of the fourth of November 1911, when the same *Le Temps* reported that *la cote retrouve toute sa fermeté* — the market recovered all its firmness — the moment the crisis resolved. A generation apart, the same anatomy: a scare priced as it builds, a floor found in a hopeful reading, a rally on the settlement, a return to ordinary business within the week. The instrument was the franc and the rente, the signal was the price of relief, and the mechanism was a market working the diplomatic news day by day and pricing, correctly and only, the crisis it could see.

That the crisis it could see was the wrong one to be relieved about is the tragedy of Munich, and it is history's point, not the market's. The market priced the peace that came. It had no power to price the war the peace was hiding, and that we now know what the men of September 1938 did not is the one advantage we hold over them — and the last one to be smug about.

## NOTES

*What the prose above sets aside — and here that is a great deal.*

**On the data.** This chapter rests on four dated *Bulletin financier* reports from the *Le Temps* run on Gallica (17 and 28 September, 30 September, 8 October 1938), plus a single exchange-rate print. The French phrases quoted are from the OCR of those finance pages and must be verified against the page images before publication. It is a qualitative market-tone arc, not a continuous price series, and no claim in the chapter should be read as resting on more than that.

**On the one number.** The 30 September exchange move — the pound from 178.90 to 178.51 francs, the dollar from 38.90 to 37.55 — is read from the day's *Changes* table in the OCR and is flagged for verification against the page image. It is a single session's quotation, the arc's only quantitative anchor.

**On the omitted issues.** Several issues across the crisis (around 22, 24, 27 September and 2, 15 October) did not surface a datable market-tone report on the finance page and are left out rather than guessed at. The arc is therefore four clean readings, not a daily line.

**On what a full study would add.** London and, after the Anschluss, the German markets; a quantified rente-and-equity series in place of the text report; the *Economist* and the *Financial Times* alongside *Le Temps*. With those, the priced war-aversion becomes a measured one. Without them, this is a proof of concept: the method — press-attributed price signal, cried-wolf structure — travels cleanly from 1911 into the interwar, and that is the claim, no larger.

**On Kirshner and causation.** Munich is the appeasement literature's home case, and this chapter supplies the priced counterpart to a war-aversion that literature asserts from stated preference. It does not supply, and the evidence does not support, the causal claim that financial caution *produced* Munich. Priced fear is a symptom of the financial community's condition, not proof that the decision bent to it. Finance was the substrate and, at most, the lever; the men made the choice.
