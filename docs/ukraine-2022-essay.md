# THE SHRUG
### Kyiv and Moscow, February 2022

*Modern companion to "The Squeeze" and "Spent." The working evidence — the reproducible FRED series and every figure flagged for verification — is in `modern_2022_russia_ukraine.md`. Notes at the end carry what the prose sets aside.*

---

On the twenty-fourth of February 2022, Russia invaded Ukraine and the S&P 500 closed higher than it had opened. The largest land war in Europe since 1945 began before dawn European time, the columns crossed at three points and the missiles hit Kyiv, and by the New York close American equity had finished the day up; within five weeks it stood above its pre-war level. The VIX rose to thirty-six — a bad week, but well short of a broken world, against the eighty-two of the COVID crash two years earlier and the eighty of 2008.

Anyone who had spent 1911 or 1912 watching the discount rate would have recognised the shape at once. The great markets were not pricing the war. They were pricing whether it would become their war, and deciding within days, correctly, that it would not.

---

## I.

The instinct when the tanks move is to look at the index, and the index is the wrong place to look. In 2022 what actually moved was one price: Brent crude went from ninety-nine dollars on the eve of the invasion to a hundred and thirty-three by the eighth of March, a third in a fortnight. That was the war as the Western market chose to express it — not a systemic threat or a solvency crisis, but a commodity shock, priced narrowly through the one channel where Russia mattered to the world economy, which was energy. Equity indexed to the invasion eve was flat to recovering while oil spiked, and that divergence is the whole finding. The market priced an energy tax and went back to worrying about the Federal Reserve.

The market was right to, in the only sense a market can be. Ukraine was catastrophic locally and narrow globally. It did not run along a web of alliances until choosing a side stopped mattering, because in 2022, unlike 1914, the choosing had already been done: NATO was not going in, and everyone knew it by the second day. A war that cannot go general is, to the great markets, a regional tragedy with a commodity attached. They discriminated exactly as the money markets of 1912 discriminated between a Balkan war that could pull in the powers and one that could not — the same test, run on daily data instead of Fraktur, returning the same answer.

---

## II.

If the violence was not in the index, it was where the exposure was: in Russia's own market, the way the French war scare of 1905 had sat not in the Paris money market but in the *fonds russes*, the Russian bonds held in French portfolios. The ruble fell from around eighty to the dollar to about a hundred and thirty-five in under two weeks, and was hauled back only by capital controls, a policy rate slammed to twenty per cent, and a demand that Europe pay for gas in rubles. The Moscow exchange closed its equity market for the better part of a month — a bourse suspending trading in a panic, the oldest signature in this book, performed in the age of the terminal. In June the Russian state entered its first foreign-currency default since 1918, the year the Bolsheviks repudiated the Tsar's debt.

The great markets shrugged and the belligerent's own market took the hit. The risk lived in the exposed instrument, and the exposed instrument in 2022 was everything denominated in the aggressor's currency and credit — not the S&P, which was never the exposure and never pretended to be.

---

## III.

There was a second thing happening, and it is the one the pre-1914 chapters can only gesture at, because in the Edwardian world finance was a condition and in 2022 it was a weapon. Within days of the invasion the G7 and the European Union froze roughly three hundred billion dollars of the Russian central bank's reserves — about half the war chest Russia had spent years accumulating to make itself sanction-proof. Major Russian banks were cut out of the SWIFT messaging system, and a wave of corporate exits and secondary-sanction pressure followed. This was not the market tightening on a government; it was governments reaching into the plumbing of the system and switching off a state's access to its own money.

The move has a lineage worth naming: the Ruhr in 1923, the freezing of Japanese assets in the summer of 1941, the run on sterling that Washington declined to arrest at Suez in 1956, and now this. The coercive use of finance, wielded by states against states, is not a modern invention. What is modern is the scale and the speed — half a great power's reserves gone before its columns reached the second Ukrainian city.

---

## IV.

The discipline that governs the rest of the book governs this chapter too, and it cuts against the drama. The reserve freeze and the sanctions did severe, measurable damage to the Russian economy; that is documented. Whether they were decisive is not. The freeze squeezed, but it did not by itself halt the invasion, which ground on for years after the money was immobilised. A weapon that inflicts great pain without stopping the enemy is a fact about capability, not outcome, and the honest sentence is the narrow one: finance was used, at unprecedented scale, it hurt, and the war continued. To say more — that the sanctions were winning or losing the war — is to claim a causal chain the evidence does not close, which is the same error the earlier chapters spend their notes refusing to make about a creditor position or a rate move.

---

## V.

2022 also confirms, by counter-example, the distinction the modern trading chapters rest on. The market did not anticipate: on the day the decision became a fact, equity rose, which is the worst forecast imaginable if a forecast was what you wanted. The moves that mattered came afterward, as the consequences priced in — oil over the following fortnight, the ruble over the following days, defense and grain and fertiliser over the following weeks. The signal was in the resolution, not the run-up. *Buy the invasion, sell the phony war*, the old desk maxim runs, and 2022 is its cleanest vindication: whoever traded the anticipation got a market that closed up on the worst news in eighty years, while whoever traded the resolution got a thirty-four per cent move in the barrel with a known start date.

The one clean safe-haven, when it came, came from outside the structure. Treasuries did not rally, because the Fed owned that market and inflation was the story. What quietly bid was the dollar — the broad index up from about a hundred and fourteen to a hundred and twenty-two, and staying there — the money the war could not reach, in the way New York's call rate fell in the autumn of 1911 while Berlin's rose. A century apart, the same asset did the same job: the instrument outside the alliance and outside the war is where frightened money goes, because it is the one place the contagion does not reach.

---

## VI.

This belongs beside Agadir and the Balkans, rather than in a separate book about a separate world, because nothing in the method had to change to read it. The same four questions — could the state fund the war, hold the consent, find the liquidity, buy the time — sort 2022 as cleanly as they sort 1912, and the discrimination test returns the same verdict a century on: even a war this large was priced by the West as a commodity shock and not a systemic threat, because it could not go general. The instrument problem holds — the index was the wrong screen, and the risk sat in the ruble and the reserve account — and the one thing the Edwardian material could only imply, finance turned from a substrate into a weapon, stands here in the open at three hundred billion dollars.

The markets of 1912 charged Austria-Hungary for a war that never came. The markets of 2022 barely charged the West for a war that did, because it came to Ukraine and to Russia and not to the system. That is one finding, stated twice a hundred and ten years apart, in the only language that keeps a continuous record of what people were actually afraid of: the price of money, the price of the barrel, and the currency the frightened reach for first.

## NOTES

*What the prose above sets aside. Every figure that is feed-grade or contested appears here.*

**On the charted series.** S&P 500, the VIX, Brent, the ten-year Treasury yield, and the broad dollar index are FRED daily series, reproducible from the identifiers in `modern_2022_russia_ukraine.md`. The invasion-day equity rise, the VIX peak of thirty-six, and the Brent move from ninety-nine to a hundred and thirty-three (23 Feb → 8 Mar) are read directly from those series.

**On the June equity low.** The S&P's June 2022 low (around 3667) was driven by the Federal Reserve and the inflation shock, not the war, and must not be attributed to Ukraine. By late March, before that low, equity had already recovered above its pre-war level.

**On the Russian-side figures.** The ruble's low near a hundred and thirty-five to the dollar (7 March), the Moscow Exchange equity closure of roughly 25 February to 24 March, and the June sovereign default (the first in foreign currency since 1918) are from the contemporary record and are flagged for verification against Central Bank of Russia, exchange, and rating-agency primary sources before publication.

**On the reserve freeze.** The figure of roughly three hundred billion dollars of Russian central-bank reserves immobilised, and the expulsion of major Russian banks from SWIFT, are from official statements and contemporary reporting and should be checked against the primary releases. The characterisation of that sum as "about half" the reserve stock is approximate.

**On decisiveness, stated against interest.** That the sanctions and the reserve freeze did severe damage to the Russian economy is documented; that they altered the course of the war is contested and is not claimed here. The freeze squeezed without halting the invasion, and the chapter holds to that sentence.

**On the safe-haven reading.** The dollar's rise (broad index ~114.7 → ~121.7) is the one clean flight-to-quality signal in the Western data; the absence of a sustained Treasury bid is attributed to Fed and inflation dynamics, an interpretation consistent with the series but not the only possible one.

**On the contemporaneous attribution.** For the record beyond the price series: the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis *Review* ("Financial Market Reactions to the Russian Invasion of Ukraine," Oct 2022) names the energy channel and attributes the equity resilience to the invasion being partly priced in — the modern counterpart to *Le Temps* or Lansburgh naming the driver in their own crises.
