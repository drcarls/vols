Independent empirical review Milk pricing & racial disparate impact September 2026

Milk Pricing Equity Audit

An independent test of two claims: that the federal Class I milk differential falls harder on Black communities, and that Walmart charges more for milk in Black neighborhoods. One is substantially supported, though mostly by freight cost rather than by anything USDA chose. One is not supported at all.

The short version, without the statistics

What the numbers in this review actually mean, and what is worth saying out loud in a first meeting.

Why would milk cost more in some places at all?

Milk is heavy and it spoils. It costs real money to truck it from dairy country in Wisconsin and upstate New York down to Atlanta or Miami. To keep milk moving to the places that don't produce it, USDA sets a minimum price that bottlers must pay, and that minimum is higher the farther you are from the cows. It is written as one dollar figure per county, from $1.70 near Eau Claire, Wisconsin to $7.40 in south Florida.

Nothing in that formula is about people. The inputs are roads, truck weight limits, fuel, labor and plant capacity. The question is whether a rule built only from freight nonetheless lands hardest on Black communities — and, if it does, whether that is the freight or the government's choices.

So does the government have an excuse? And is it any good?

Yes, and mostly yes. USDA relies on a computer model that works out what milk is worth in every county in the country using nothing but distance and logistics. That model's actual county-by-county numbers were filed at the rulemaking hearing and accepted into evidence, so they can be checked rather than argued about.

Checked, they say this: if you priced milk purely on trucking cost, Black communities would pay about 4.7¢ more per gallon than white ones. The rule that was actually in force from 2008 to 2025 produced a gap of 3.8¢ — smaller than pure geography would have produced.

So the disparity is real. Black Americans really do live disproportionately in the expensive-milk half of the country. But for those seventeen years you cannot pin it on USDA's choices, because USDA was being gentler than the map. If the claim is that the old schedule discriminated, the government puts this model on the table and the claim loses.

Then what changed in 2025?

USDA rewrote the price map. Two separate things happened, and only one of them matters.

It raised the price everywhere. This is the part the rule was justified by, and it did nothing at all to the racial gap — adding the same amount to everyone's price doesn't make anything more unequal.

It also changed which places pay more than which. This is where the entire increase in the disparity came from. The gap went from 3.8¢ to 5.6¢, and every bit of that came from re-sloping the map rather than from raising it. Measured against USDA's own freight model, somewhere between half and five-sixths of that re-sloping has no support in the cost figures the government itself relies on.

That is the finding: the new map goes past what the trucking costs, and where it goes past it, it falls harder on Black communities than the old map did.

Three things to get ahead of, because the other side will find them
  1. The above-cost piece is not statistically solid. It points the same direction under every test and it never reverses — but it does not clear the conventional bar. Anything filed describing it as proven will be taken apart by a competent government expert. "Consistent but not conclusive" is the defensible phrasing.
  2. The story is not "USDA charged Black neighborhoods more." Detroit is 37% Black and is priced below what the freight model says milk is worth there. So is Chicago. Philadelphia sits within a dime of it. What actually happened is that USDA priced California well below cost and Texas, Florida and New England above it — and California is the least-Black of the big regions, so a racial pattern falls out of a regional decision. Better to say this yourself than to have it said to you.
  3. Where it does bite is the rural Black Belt. The Blackest counties in the country get marked up the most above freight cost, and that pattern is clear and consistent. Those counties simply hold too few people to move the national average, which is exactly why the headline test comes back inconclusive.

So what is actually worth bringing?

Not the statistics — the paperwork. Every item below is a document, not an estimate:

USDA said this rule moves $4 billion a year, then analyzed nobody who pays it. No breakdown by income, geography or race. None at all.

Its civil-rights sign-off is three sentences, and it discusses only dairy farmers — not the households buying the milk. USDA's own directive says that review is supposed to cover program participants, including WIC and SNAP recipients.

A milk processor told them in writing that this would land on low-income households and food-assistance recipients. The final rule does not answer it.

Four different price maps were on the table, and USDA picked the one that falls hardest on Black communities — with its own freight model not supporting the difference.

And the map was not calculated; it was negotiated. The dairy industry coalition that drafted it describes its own method in a hearing exhibit: pick nineteen anchor cities, have regional subgroups raise or lower the model's numbers using local knowledge, move individual counties between price zones so that named bottling plants don't pay more than the competitors they bid against, then smooth the result until it looked tidy and explainable.

None of that requires a statistician. It is: you moved four billion dollars a year, you were told in writing who it would hurt, and you answered in three sentences.

One caveat that belongs on all of it. The freight figures used here come from a March 2016 run of the model. The 2025 map was built on 2021 runs — the industry quotes their results but never filed the underlying county table. Obtaining it is the single most valuable step left, and it is a narrow, well-identified request.

Supported — between states

Finding A · Federal Class I differential

Areas with more Black residents sit under systematically higher federal minimum milk prices. A 13.5¢/gallon gap, robust to income and population controls. The gradient is between states and regions, not within them — and most of it tracks USDA's own least-cost transport model, which is now testable and which the government will rely on.

Not supported

Finding B · Walmart retail pricing

No racial gradient in Walmart milk prices survives any geographic control. In Atlanta — the best-powered test available — the effect is −2.9¢, p = 0.65, with power to detect anything the size of the claim.

Established, but not a disparity

Collateral · How milk is priced

Fluid milk is priced store-by-store while adjacent dairy is priced nationally — confirmed across three datasets and two retailers. A mechanism capable of producing disparity; not evidence of one.

Scope

What was tested

Two independent claims, tested separately against public data. Neither test depends on the other.

Finding A concerns a federal rule. Under 7 CFR 1000.52 USDA sets a location-specific minimum price processors must pay for milk destined for drinking — the Class I differential — as one dollar figure per county, ranging from $1.70/cwt near Eau Claire, Wisconsin to $7.40/cwt in south Florida. Its inputs are entirely supply-side: hauling distance, plant capacity, road weight limits. No input is demographic. The question is whether the resulting cost surface nonetheless falls harder on Black communities.

Finding B concerns a company. The claim is that Walmart charges more for milk in Black neighborhoods than in comparable white ones.

Prices are quoted per hundredweight ($/cwt) in the federal rule and per gallon at retail; the conversion used throughout is 11.63 gal/cwt.

A note on what this document is

This is an independent statistical review, not legal advice and not an expert report. It tests whether the empirical claims hold, using public data and pre-specified designs. Where a result does not survive, that is reported as prominently as where it does.

Finding A

The federal differential does fall harder on Black communities

And it does so at a magnitude that is easy to state: about thirteen and a half cents on every gallon.

Comparing ZIP codes at the two ends of the racial distribution, the federal minimum price is $1.57/cwt higher where 30% or more of residents are Black than where 10% or fewer are — 13.5¢ per gallon, or roughly $14 per household per year at typical consumption.

Read that ladder carefully, because it is the whole case. The disparity is large and highly robust to income — adding median income and population moves the coefficient by only 2%. It survives comparison across census regions, marginally. It does not survive comparison within a single state.

That pattern is intrinsic to the rule rather than a defect in the analysis: 95% of all variation in the Class I differential is between states, because it is a national geographic cost surface. There is very little within-state variation for a within-state test to use.

The rebuttal counsel should expect

"This is geography, not race. Black Americans are concentrated in the Southeast, which is far from the Upper Midwest dairy supply. The rule prices distance."

That rebuttal is partly right, and it can now be tested directly rather than argued around — USDA's own cost model was entered into the rulemaking record, and the section below measures the differential against it. First, the weaker form of the answer: the gradient does not vanish when the South is removed. It appears inside every census region, though only the Northeast is individually significant once errors are clustered.

The defensible claim at this stage is: a real, substantial, income-robust disparity in a federal price floor, operating at the level of states and regions rather than neighborhoods. The next section asks how much of it a least-cost freight model produces on its own.

Finding A · the decisive test

Measured against USDA's own cost model, the geography defense largely holds

The least-cost freight surface is itself racially graded — and by more than the differentials that were in force before 2025. This is reported first because it is the result that most constrains the case.

USDA's Class I differentials are built from the U.S. Dairy Sector Simulator, a transshipment model that finds the cheapest way to move raw milk from farms to plants and finished product to consumers, subject to road networks, state truck weight limits, plant capacity, and regional fuel and labor costs. Its output includes a shadow price for milk at every location — a pure cost surface with no demographic input of any kind.

That surface was filed at the national rulemaking hearing by the model's own caretaker and admitted into evidence: a 3,085-county table of Class I and Class III shadow prices. It can be compared, county by county, against what USDA actually adopted. The comparison is clean, because the statistic used here — the person-weighted Black mean minus the person-weighted White mean — is mathematically unchanged by shifting a whole surface up or down, and a shadow-price surface has no meaningful zero. Only the shape of each surface is being compared, which is the only thing they share.

What this means for the case, stated plainly

For the schedule that stood from 2008 to 2025, the geography defense is not a rationalization — it is correct. Least-cost dairy logistics, run with no demographic input, produce a larger racial gap (4.68¢) than the differentials actually in force (3.82¢). On this measure the old schedule was less racially graded than freight economics alone would dictate. Counsel should assume the government makes this argument and wins it.

What survives is narrower and better aimed at the 2025 amendment, which is the first schedule to price above the cost surface in aggregate incidence.

The 2025 amendment: the increase did not cause the widening

The 2025 amendment raised the population-weighted differential by $1.27/cwt and widened the racial gap from 3.82¢ to 5.59¢. Those two facts are unrelated. Because the gap is unchanged by shifting a surface uniformly, none of the increase in level produced any of the widening. All 1.78¢ of it came from re-sloping — changing which counties pay more relative to which. And most of the re-sloping has no support in USDA's own model:

The argument is only ever about the last quarter — and there the record shows the surface was negotiated, not computed. The producer coalition that drafted it describes, in its own hearing exhibit, choosing nineteen “anchor cities,” then convening regional subgroups instructed to increase or decrease the model-generated values using local knowledge, then moving individual counties between price zones to equalize raw-product costs among named competing bottling plants, then “fine tuning” the result into an “explainable and contiguous” surface. That is a documented fact about the process, and it does not depend on any statistic.

Where the honest limit of the statistics falls

The 0.91¢–1.53¢ that exceeds the cost surface is not statistically significant once errors are handled properly. Under the same state-block permutation that validates the headline gap, the excess-over-cost returns p = 0.289 against the shadow price and p = 0.106 against the give-up charge. County-level regressions of the departure on percent Black give coefficients that are consistently positive and stable — no single state's removal flips the sign, in 48 leave-one-out runs — but reach only t +1.66 and t +2.09, with permutation p of 0.212 and 0.113.

Point estimates consistently positive, never significant is the accurate summary. Any filing that characterizes the above-cost markup as a demonstrated racial gradient will be impeached on this point.

And the mechanism is not the intuitive one

The positive gradient is driven by a California-versus-South-and-Northeast contrast rather than a Black-versus-white contrast within cities. USDA prices the California metros far below what its cost model says milk is worth there, and Texas, Florida and New England above it:

The administrative record

The strongest material is procedural, not statistical

Four findings from the rulemaking record for the 2025 amendment. None of them requires the racial gradient to clear a significance threshold.

1 · USDA quantified the transfer and analyzed nobody who pays it

USDA's own Regulatory Economic Impact Analysis puts the amendment's annual transfer at $4.01 billion. It declined to model the incidence of that transfer, and contains no consumer analysis of any kind — no distributional breakdown by income, geography, or race.

2 · The Civil Rights Impact Analysis is three sentences

USDA's own directive on civil-rights review covers program beneficiaries, including WIC and SNAP recipients. The certification accompanying this rule disposes of the question in three sentences, and addresses only dairy operators — not the households who buy the milk. For comparison, the analysis accompanying Federal Order Reform in 1999–2000 ran to 131 pages, though it too was industry-scoped.

3 · Consumer incidence was raised on the record and went unanswered

A commenter representing fluid-milk processors put the burden on lower-income consumers and nutrition-program participants squarely before the agency. The final rule does not respond to it. Failure to respond to a significant comment is ordinary Administrative Procedure Act territory and does not require proving any disparity at all.

4 · A less discriminatory alternative was on the table

Four county-level schedules were before USDA. Holding the national average level equal so that only distribution differs, the processor coalition's filed schedule carries a 2.62¢ gap, the pre-existing schedule 3.36¢, the producer proposal 4.60¢, and the schedule USDA adopted 5.05¢ — the most racially graded of the four options in front of it. USDA also cut the producer proposal down in 2,153 counties and raised it in 419, and its pattern of cuts was smaller where the Black share was higher (t +12.08).

The qualification that belongs beside finding 4

The alternative schedule is weaker than it first appears. It is the pre-existing surface shifted down by a uniform $1.60, so its shape is the old shape — which, as the section above establishes, is flatter than the cost model implies. USDA has grounds in the record to reject it for under-signaling location. A less discriminatory alternative that tracks the cost surface rather than falling below it would be a materially stronger one, and constructing it is the open analytical task.

One procedural obstacle counsel will already have in mind

Under Block v. Community Nutrition Institute, 467 U.S. 340 (1984), judicial review of milk marketing orders is confined to handlers — consumers were held to lack standing to challenge them directly. The commenter who raised consumer incidence represents handlers. That is the procedural vehicle question, and it is a question for counsel.

Finding B

The Walmart retail claim does not survive

It appears under the original design, and disappears under every attempt to control for geography. The strongest available test returns a precise zero.

The original analysis compared high-Black and low-Black rural ZIP codes matched on income and population, and found positive gaps in four states. The difficulty is that in those states the two groups almost never share a county — typically 1 to 4 counties out of 25 to 39 contain both. The comparison is therefore between different local markets, which differ in freight, store format, and competition as well as race.

The decisive test: a single metropolitan market

The objection to every result above is symmetrical — controlling for geography might remove the very mechanism at issue, while not controlling leaves the comparison confounded. A large metro breaks that deadlock. Fulton and Clayton counties in Atlanta are 67–68% Black; Cherokee and Forsyth are under 7%. All sit in one metropolitan market, one labor market, one competitive environment, and two adjacent Class I zones.

Why this null is informative rather than merely inconclusive

Atlanta has more high-Black stores (30) than any single state's rural sample in the original analysis, with a matched comparison group in the same market. It would detect a 28¢ gap with over 99% power and a 16¢ gap with 95% power. It finds −2.9¢.

Across twelve metropolitan areas, not one shows a significant positive gradient; pooled with metro fixed effects the coefficient is −0.0007 (t −0.88). By county, DeKalb (44% Black), Gwinnett (27%) and Forsyth (7%) price within one cent of each other; Atlanta's expensive stores are its outer exurban ring.

Recommendation: the retail pricing theory should not be advanced on this evidence. It fails in the jurisdiction it was built on, fails in the best-powered test available, and fails nationally.

Collateral findings

Three things the data does establish

Each is solid, and none of them is a racial disparity. They matter because they constrain what can be argued.

1 · Milk is priced differently from everything beside it

Across a 13-item Great Value basket at all 92 South Carolina Walmart stores, whole milk took 25 distinct prices while sour cream, mozzarella, Greek yogurt, eggs and canned green beans each took exactly one statewide. The pattern reproduces in a national Walmart sample and independently at Aldi.

This establishes a mechanism — fluid milk is managed store-by-store while adjacent dairy is priced nationally — and forecloses the defense that all traffic-driver goods are priced locally, since bread and eggs are not. It is a necessary predicate for any pricing theory. It is not itself evidence of disparity.

2 · The affordability gap is real and it is income, not price

Milk cost as a share of household income rises steeply with the Black share of a ZIP: +0.0029 percentage points per point (t +11.9) with state fixed effects. But the price coefficient over the same stores is negative (−0.0037, t −7.1), while median income falls by $359 per percentage point (t −12.9).

The disparity is entirely the denominator. That is a genuine and large finding about a necessity, but bread or rent would produce the same coefficient — it is a statement about income inequality, not about anyone's pricing conduct.

3 · State regulation moves milk prices more than anything else measured

Pennsylvania and New Jersey set minimum retail milk prices by statute. Stores there average $4.75/gal against $3.47 where no such regime applies — +$1.28, three times the largest geographic contrast in this data and sixteen times the retail effect originally claimed. Those states, plus four with classified-pricing programs reaching retail, are excluded from the panel above.

Notably, Pennsylvania — the highest-priced state in the country — has a Walmart footprint that is 2.6% Black at the median against a state population near 12%. High milk prices are not, in themselves, a racial phenomenon.

Next steps

What would change the picture

  • The 2021 model run the adopted schedule was actually built on. The cost surface tested above is a March 2016 run — the one that reached the record. The producer coalition worked from May and October 2021 runs, quotes their results, and did not attach the county table. Obtaining it would replace every vintage caveat in this review and is the single highest-value discovery target.
  • Whether a completed civil-rights worksheet exists behind the three-sentence certification. USDA's directive contemplates one. A Freedom of Information Act request would establish either that the analysis was done and not disclosed, or that the certification rests on nothing.
  • A less discriminatory alternative that tracks the cost surface. The alternative already in the record falls below it and is rejectable on that ground. A schedule that matches the model's shape at the adopted level would remove the above-cost component without weakening the locational signal — this is constructible from the data in hand.
  • The 49-day hearing transcript. Not yet reviewed. It would contain USDA's stated reasons for departing from the model, and any consumer testimony given.
  • Walmart's price-zone assignment records. If the retail theory is revisited at all, the challengeable practice is the assignment of stores to price groups — a centralized, documented decision — not store-level pricing conduct, which this analysis finds no racial gradient in.
  • Independent verification of the retail price series. The shelf prices analyzed here come from a single collection pipeline that this review was unable to reproduce independently; commercial scraping tools return national online prices rather than shelf prices.

Method. Store-level Walmart prices joined to Census ACS tables B03002 (race) and B19013 (median household income) by ZIP, and to the USDA Class I differential by county. Analyses use ordinary least squares with income and log population controls; fixed effects at county, postal-region, state or census-region level as stated; standard errors clustered on state where a state-level surface is the object. Matched-pair tests use nearest-neighbor matching on income and log population with one-sided permutation inference. Eight states are excluded from the national panel: Pennsylvania and New Jersey (statutory minimum retail milk prices), Maine, North Dakota, Virginia and Montana (classified pricing reaching retail), and Alaska and Hawaii (non-contiguous).

Sources for the federal-rule sections. County differentials, the four competing schedules, and the cost-model shadow prices are all public documents from USDA's national Federal Milk Marketing Order pricing hearing and the resulting 2025 final rule; the cost surface is a hearing exhibit admitted into evidence, sponsored by one of the model's two caretakers. County demographics are ACS 2023 table B03002. Cost and regulatory surfaces are compared only on distributional shape, using a statistic that is mathematically invariant to shifting a surface uniformly — a shadow-price surface has no meaningful zero, so its level is never differenced against a regulatory level.

Limitations. The retail sample covers only ZIP codes containing a Walmart, which is not a random sample of American neighborhoods and under-represents dense urban areas. Class I results on the Walmart-ZIP sample are corroboration, not a reproduction of a county-universe analysis; the county-universe figures are reported separately and are the ones to quote. The cost-model run in the record is a single month (March 2016) and the adopted schedule was built on 2021 data, so some divergence between them is vintage rather than method — though vintage cannot generate a gradient in racial composition unless freight costs themselves moved with it. Exceeding the cost surface is not unlawful: the governing statute directs USDA to weigh producer returns and orderly marketing alongside cost, so “above the model” is evidence about the rule's stated rationale, not a legal conclusion. The 49-day hearing transcript has not been reviewed.
