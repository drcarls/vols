## 1. The memo's core claim is confirmed

The entire Civil Rights Impact Analysis in the final rule is **three sentences, 899
characters**, reproduced here in full:

> **Civil Rights Impact Analysis**
>
> AMS has reviewed this rulemaking in accordance with USDA Departmental Regulation 4300–004,
> Civil Rights Impact Analysis, to identify any major civil rights impacts the rule might have
> on **FMMO participants** on the basis of race, color, national origin, disability, sex, gender
> identity, political beliefs, age, marital, family/parental status, religion, sexual
> orientation, reprisal, **or because an individual's income is derived from any public
> assistance program**. Based on the review and analysis of the rule and all available data,
> issuance of this final rule is not likely to negatively impact low and moderate-income
> populations, minority populations, women, Tribes or persons with disabilities, by virtue of
> their age, race, color, national origin, sex, disability, or marital or familial status. No
> major civil rights impact is likely to result from this final rule.

No supporting analysis, data, table or citation accompanies it. For comparison, the **Regulatory
Flexibility Act** analysis in the same rule runs past **20,000 characters**.

## 3. The comparator is damning on rigour — but not on scope

USDA performed a **131-page** CRIA for the 1999–2000 Federal Order Reform, with quantitative
tables by marketing area drawn from the Census of Agriculture. So the agency has done a
substantive FMMO civil-rights analysis before, and the 2025 three-sentence certification is a
sharp departure in **process**.

**But the historical CRIA does not support a consumer-scope argument, and counsel must know
this.** Classifying its 108 "minority" mentions by context:

| What the mention concerns | Count |
|---|---|
| minority **producers / dairy operators** | 51 |
| unclassified | 55 |
| **consumers / beneficiaries** | **2** |

Its actual analysis asks whether minority *dairy operators* reach 5% of operators in each
marketing area — e.g. *"Minority males represent at least five percent of all dairy operators in
5 of the 32 marketing areas, 1.3 percent for all markets combined."* It contains **no consumer
incidence analysis**, no "low-income," no WIC, and one passing mention of food stamps.

**USDA has never treated milk consumers as the CRIA population for FMMOs.** That consistency
is a defence, not a vulnerability: an agency applying a long-settled (if contestable) reading of
its own directive is on stronger APA footing than one that changed course.

## 4. Where the argument is actually strongest

Two footholds survive the above, and they are narrower and better than the version in the memo.

**(a) The rule invokes a standard it then does not apply.** The certification expressly names
*"because an individual's income is derived from any public assistance program"* as a protected
basis. That basis is meaningless for producers and handlers — dairy farmers and milk processors
do not derive income from public assistance. It only has content if the affected population
includes **consumers who do**. AMS recited a beneficiary-facing standard, scoped its review to
"FMMO participants," and produced no analysis of either. That mismatch is the sharpest available
APA "failed to consider an important aspect of the problem" argument.

**(b) The statute names consumers.** The AMAA's purposes — as quoted in USDA's own 1999 CRIA —
include *"(4) assure consumers of adequate supplies of good quality milk at reasonable
prices."* And the CRIA framework's own question **1c** asks whether groups have been excluded
"among the **beneficiary or participant** population." Consumers are within both the statute's
purposes and the directive's vocabulary; they are simply absent from the analysis.

## 5. What this does and does not establish

**Established by document inspection:**
- The CRIA is a three-sentence conclusory certification with no supporting analysis.
- The rule contains no consumer, retail, household, WIC, SNAP, low-income, incidence or
  distributional language whatsoever.
- The same rule devotes 20,000+ characters to small-entity economic impact.
- USDA produced a 131-page CRIA for the analogous 1999–2000 action.

**Not established:**
- That USDA was legally *required* to analyse consumer incidence. The AMAA mandate is
  producer-focused, FMMOs regulate handlers only, and USDA's consistent past practice has scoped
  FMMO CRIAs to industry participants. This is contested ground, not settled ground.
- That a court would find the certification inadequate. Conclusory civil-rights certifications
  are common across agencies and frequently survive review.
- Anything about the 2025 figures themselves, the WIC analysis, or the memo's §6
  alternative-schedule counterfactual. **Still unaudited — and the §6 counterfactual remains the
  load-bearing piece**, because a disparate-impact theory generally needs a less discriminatory
  alternative and the incidence statistic does not supply one.

## 6. Recommended next steps

1. **Pull the full docket comments** (AMS-DA-23-0031 / the national FMMO pricing hearing record)
   to establish whether *anyone* raised consumer price incidence on the record. If a commenter
   did and AMS did not respond, the APA argument strengthens considerably. If nobody did,
   exhaustion and waiver problems arise.
2. **Verify the §6 counterfactual.** This is the highest-value remaining analytical task.
3. **Obtain the CRIA worksheet itself**, if one exists beyond the Federal Register paragraph, via
   FOIA to AMS. The directive contemplates a documented analysis; whether a completed worksheet
   exists is a discoverable fact and a good FOIA target.
4. **Note the vehicle.** With *Alexander v. Sandoval* (2001) foreclosing a private right of
   action on Title VI disparate-impact regulations, the realistic routes are APA review, an
   administrative civil-rights complaint to USDA OASCR, a rulemaking petition, or comments on the
   next amendment cycle — not a private disparate-impact suit. Counsel should confirm.
